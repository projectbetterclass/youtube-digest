¨ I think that all investors would buy into the precept 
that investing in equities comes with risk, but that is 
where the consensus seems to end. 
¨ Everything else about risk is contested, starting with 
whether it is a good or a bad, whether it should be 
sought out or avoided, and how it should be 
measured.

Type of Risk/Uncertainty Description Effect on value and investing
Estimation versus Economic • Estimation uncertainty reflects the possibility 
that you could have the “wrong model” or 
estimated inputs incorrectly within this 
model.
• Economic uncertainty comes the fact that 
markets and economies can change over time 
and that even the best medals will fail to 
capture these unexpected changes.
Estimation uncertainty can be reduced 
or even eliminated by collecting more 
data or building bigger models. 
Economic uncertainty will remain no 
matter how hard you try.
Micro versus Macro • Micro uncertainty refers to uncertainty about 
the potential market for a firm’s products, the 
competition it will face and the quality of its 
management team.
• Macro uncertainty reflects the reality that 
your firm’s fortunes can be affected by 
changes in the macro economic environment.
Exposure to micro uncertainty can be 
reduced by holding a more diverse 
portfolio, but exposure to macro 
uncertainty is immune from 
diversification (though it can be hedged 
at a cost or avoided).
Discrete versus Continuous • Discrete risk: Risks that lie dormant for 
periods but show up at points in time (eg. 
Regulatory approval or disapproval, 
devaluation of a fixed exchange rate currency
• Continuous risk: Risks that occur continuously 
and affect value as they happen.
Continuous risk can be incorporated into 
required returns (or discount rates) in 
valuation, but discrete risks cannot. They 
need probabilistic approaches like 
decision trees.

path of the asset and what you can extract from that 
path as a measure of risk. 
¨ Earnings/Cashflow Measures: There are many

Price Based Risk Measures
Aswath Damodaran
7
¨ The most widely accessible measures of risk come 
from the market, for publicly traded stocks, where 
trading and liquidity generate prices that change 
with each trade. 
¨ That price data is then used to extract risk measures, 
ranging from intuitive ones (high to low ranges) to 
statistical measures (such as standard deviation and 
covariance).
8
1. High Low Risk Measure
¨ To compute a risk measure from high and low prices that 
is comparable across stocks, the range has to be scaled 
to the price level. One simple scalar is the sum of the 
high and the low prices, giving the following measure of 
risk:
HiLo Risk = (High Price - Low Price)/ (High Price + Low Price)
¨ To illustrate, consider two stocks, A with a high of $50 
and a low of $25 and B with a high of $12 and a low of 
$8. The risk measures computed will be:
¤ HiLo Risk of stock A = (50-25)/ (50+25) = 0.333
¤ HiLo Risk of stock B = (12-8)/ (12 +8) = 0.20

Pluses and Minuses
¨ Pluses 
¤ Simple to compute and requires minimal data. 
¤ It is even intuitive, especially if you track market prices 
continuously. 
¨ Minuses 
¤ It throws away all data other than the high and the low 
price. 
¤ For the price to move, there has to be trading, and markets 
that are liquid will therefore see more price movements, 
especially over shorter time period, than markets that are 
not.
11
2. Standard Deviation in Stock Prices
¨ As with the HiLo risk measure, computing a standard 
deviation in stock prices, without adjusting for price 
levels, would yield the unsurprising conclusion that 
higher prices stocks have higher standard deviations. 
¨ With this measure, the scaling adjustment becomes a 
simpler one, since using percentage price changes, 
instead of prices themselves, should level the playing 
field. 
¨ In fact, if you wanted a fully integrated measure of 
returns, you should also include dividends in the periods 
where you receive them. However, since dividends get 
paid, at most, once every quarter, analysts who use daily 
or weekly returns often ignore them.

Pluses of Standard Deviation
¤ It is Statistics 101. 
¤ It can be computed for returns in any asset class, thus 
allowing us to compare it across stocks, high yield bonds, 
corporate bonds, real estate or crypto currencies. 
¤ To the extent that we can also compute historical returns 
on these same assets, it allows us to relate those returns to 
the standard deviations and compute the payoff to taking 
risk in the form of Sharpe ratios or information ratios.
14
Minuses of Standard Deviation
¨ Not Normal: To the extent that investment return 
distributions are skewed (often with long positive tails and 
sometimes with long negative tails), we know that we should 
be looking at other moments in the distribution.
¨ Upside versus Downside Variance: It will higher if you have 
outsized returns, whether they are higher or lower than the 
average. 
¨ Liquidity effects: As with the high low risk measure, liquidity 
plays a role in how volatile a stock is.
¨ Total Risk, rather than risk added to a portfolio: The standard 
deviation in stock prices measures the total risk in a stock, 
rather than how much risk it adds to a portfolio.

Earnings Risk Measures
¨ For many value investors, the biggest problem with 
using standard deviations or betas is that they come 
from stock prices. 
¨ In the value world, it is not markets that should drive 
our perception of risk, but the fundamentals of the 
company. Thus, using a price based risk measure 
when doing intrinsic value is viewed as inconsistent.

Pluses and Minuses
¨ While I sympathize with the argument that value investors 
pose, i.e., that using price based risk measures in intrinsic 
valuation is inconsistent, the accounting tendency to smooth 
things out shows up in earnings streams.
¨ If you add to that how the numerous discretionary accounting 
plays play out in stated earnings, I am not sure that I learn 
much about risk from looking at a time series of accounting 
earnings. 
¨ You may find that there are other items in accounting 
statements that are less susceptible to accounting choices, 
such as revenues or cash flows, but I remain unconvinced 
that any of these beat price-based measures of risk.

Risk Proxies
¨ The vast majority of investors never attach risk 
measures to stocks, choosing instead to proxies or 
stand-ins for risk. 
¤ Thus, tech stocks are viewed as riskier than non-tech 
stocks, small cap stocks are perceived as more risky than 
large cap stocks.
¤ In some value investing circles, stocks that trade at low PE 
ratios or have high dividend yields are viewed as safer than 
stocks with high PE ratios or do not pay dividends.

Picking your Poison
1. Risk avoidance is not a strategy: Avoiding risk is less a risk 
strategy than a desperation ploy. In investing, the objective 
is to maximize returns, with risk operating as a constraint. In 
corporate finance, this lesson has been forgotten by risk 
managers, where the focus has been on products (hedging, 
derivatives) that companies can use to minimize risk 
exposure.
2. Disagree with models, don't abandon first principles: If you 
don’t like a risk model, please do not throw the baby out 
with the bathwater and abandon first principles. 
3. Pick the risk measure that is right for you: You should look 
inwards, because you have to find a risk measure that works 
for you.
