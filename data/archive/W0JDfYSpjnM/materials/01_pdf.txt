§While there is much to be learned from modern portfolio 
theory, one of its more deleterious consequences has been a 
dumbing down of the cost of equity, where intuition and 
common sense are supplanted by heated arguments about 
the capital asset pricing model and betas. 
§To bring the discussion back to good sense, it is better to start 
with a recognition that the cost of equity for an investor 
should reflect what investors can earn elsewhere in the 
market on an investment of equivalent risk and understand 
that is what all these models try to measure, often imperfectly. 
§That number is subjective can vary across investors, based 
on risk aversion, but the requirement that you can earn that 
number elsewhere creates a reality check.

§One reason that the Anthropic cost of capital discussion may 
have been hijacked is the almost mindless use of debt in 
funding data centers, a practice that I have strongly attacked as 
senseless for both the borrowers and for the lenders. 
§With Coreweave as poster child, this has led to an explosion of 
concerns about default risk and in market-implied default rates and 
spreads for some companies. 
§By extension, Anthropic has become part of this discussion because of 
the compute commitments that it has locked in, for good and bad 
reasons. 
§In fact, the argument for a higher cost of capital goes as follows. If 
it costs Anthropic 10% or more to borrow money today , it 
should have a much higher cost of equity to reflect the 
residual risk. 
§That intuition breaks down for a simple reason. The cost of debt 
for companies in this space is high, because of worries about 
default, and the cost of debt in the cost of capital calculation is 
designed for going concerns.

§My estimate for the cost of debt for Anthropic reflects their 
bond rating of BB+, and it is not a value destroyer for two 
reasons. 
§First, Anthropic may owe a lot in commitments, but they are spread 
out over time, with about $60 billion coming due next year and $76 
billion the year after. Those are large commitments, but Anthropic 
has a cash balance of $114 billion, which will increase to over 
$200 billion after the IPO. 
§Second, I am not ignoring the risk of default, because I am 
incorporating a failure risk of 10%, where Anthropic does run 
into headwinds, is unable to meet its contractual obligations and has 
to make fire sale. If your concerns about default are higher, that is 
the number you should be moving, not the cost of capital. 
§In fact, if you ignore failure risk, as many analysts do, and 
try to jam default risk it into cost of capital, you would need 
a cost of capital of about 11% to arrive at an estimate of value 
close to mine.

§It is true, based on this table, that you could push the cost of 
capital for Anthropic higher, perhaps towards the 90th 
percentile of technology firms, and give it a cost of capital 
of 12%, but there are two caveats. 
§The first is that you would then have to set the failure rate to 0%, 
since the costs of capital in this table do not adjust for failure (and 
especially so at the higher risk percentiles). 
§The second is that your cost of capital, in your valuation, has to 
change over time to be consistent with your story line for 
Anthropic as a company . 
§Thus, if you see Anthropic scaling up revenues over time 
and profits turning positive, its cost of capital will move 
towards a more typical cost of capital for tech companies over 
time. If you don't see that happening, you don't need a high 
cost of capital to conclude that Anthropic is worth little or 
even nothing.

§ I know that there are analysts and investors out there who 
have picked a required rate of return as their hurdle rate 
for investing and never revisit that number, and almost as many 
CFOs who do the same for the hurdle rates at companies. 
§With individual investors and privately owned businesses, 
the consequences of setting an artificially high cost of capital

§With institutional investors and publicly traded companies,

§While it is true that company hurdle rates are for internal use and not often 
visible from the outside, surveys of CFOs, asking them about what the 
costs of capital are for their company, often come back with the conclusion 
that these hurdle are set about 2-3% higher than justified by market 
numbers. 
§Again, you may be tempted to let this practice go, as just being 
conservative, and protecting companies from taking bad investments. Here 
again, though, there is a trade off. For companies that estimate returns on 
projects rigorously, having a higher hurdle rate than appropriate will 
mean the good investments will be left untaken. 
§Early in a company's life, when project choices are plentiful, this may not 
be a deal breaker, but as companies mature, it will cost investors in the 
company, as the company holds on to cash and does not invest. 
§There is another, and potentially an even more dangerous consequence 
that undermines the entire process of investment analysis. When 
hurdle rates are set unrealistically high, relative to what you can earn in the 
market, managers, recognizing that they will never find investments that 
earn more than those hurdle rates will start to skew cashflows and growth 
upwards to compensate for discount rates being too high.

§If you stick with unrealistically high hurdle rates. you will find 
it difficult, if not impossible, to find equities that can deliver 
those rates. 
§I can assure you that with a 20% cost of equity , for instance, there is 
not one firm in the S&P 500 that will be undervalued for you to invest 
in, and if you do find firms that look undervalued in the broader market, 
it is because they are basket-case firms on the verge of tipping over. 
§Thus, a reasonable and logical follow-up would be to reduce or even 
eliminate your equity exposure in your portfolio, though if your 
unreasonableness on hurdle rates carries over into other asset classes, 
you will find no risky asset class that meets your rate of return criteria.
§More dangerously, it will leave you open to scams, where your 
search for high return investments will lead you invest with a 
"friend of a friend" or a "Y ouTube investment celebrity", who will 
claim to have found a way to deliver more than your hurdle rate 
with investing alchemy.

§For businesses with private owners, setting the hurdle rate 
too high will lead them to be more conservative in taking 
investments, opening the door to more aggressive 
competition, often from publicly traded companies or private 
business competitors with more reasonable hurdle rates. 
§The bigger danger here again is when you get ready to sell, 
you will sell your business for too low a price. You will 
facilitated in this process by private company appraisers who 
come up with costs of capital that are outlandishly high, 
usually getting there with what they call the "build up" 
approach, a nonsensical piling on of premiums that are either 
thinly sourced (a small cap premium that ceases to exist in 
1981), double counted (an illiquidity premium, with an 
additional illiquidity discount of value) or just made up to (a 
non a company-specific risk premium, where a number is 
added on to get to whatever discount rate you want).
