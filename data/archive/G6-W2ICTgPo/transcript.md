So here's the basic principle for weighting equity and debt in the cost of capital. They should always be market value numbers. As opposed to what? As opposed to book value numbers. The reason I emphasize that is half of all companies actually still use book value weights to come up with cost of capital. And there are lots of reasons CFO and others give for still sticking with book value weights. One is that book value is more reliable than market value. And they base it on the fact that book value is actually not as volatile. That's true, book value doesn't change as much as market value, but it doesn't change as much because accountants actually re estimate book value only once every three months if you're a US company, and perhaps even less frequently if you're a non-US company. The fact that something does not move does not make it more reliable. The second argument you hear is that they're just being more conservative. But using book value weights is actually less conservative for most companies 'cause it'll give you a lower cost of capital, and here's why: Book value weight will weight debt more and equity less and the cost of debt is usually much lower than the cost of equity. The third reason people give is that their return in equity in capital numbers, these are accounting return numbers, are based on book value and that's absolutely true. And because they're based in book value numbers they argue that the cost of capital should also be based in book value numbers. That's nonsense. The cost of capital is a very different concept, it's the cost of raising funding from the market today. Your book values are completely irrelevant when it comes to cost of capital. So in computing cost of capital you need market value equity and market value debt. With the publicly traded company the market value should be relatively simple to get, right? You've got a share price, you've got number of shares. So in most cases getting a market value of equity is easy. Market value of debt is usually trickier because most companies, even those with bonds outstanding, have a significant amount of debt that's bank debt and that's not trade. So what most bankers and most analysts tend to do is use book value of debt as a proxy for market value of debt while using market value of equity in the cost of capital weights. So here's the final part of the process. Once you have the cost of equity and cost of capital the question is what next? And in this case you might be a little puzzled because we have described both these numbers as hurdle rates. "How can they both be hurdle rates?" You might ask. Take Disney, the cost of capital is 7.81%, the cost of equity is much higher. You pick the higher of the two, the lower of the two, some average of the two? The answer actually is surprisingly simple. You tell me how you compute returns and I'll tell you what the right hurdle rate is. And here's how it's going to work, If you measure returns to equity investors, return in equity, cash flows to equity, cash flows left over after debt payments then the appropriate debt hurdle rate is the cost of equity. If you measure returns to the entire business, returns on capital using pre-debt earnings, then the appropriate hurdle rate is the cost of capital. You cannot mix and match. You tell me how you measure cash flows or returns and I'll tell you what the right hurdle rate to use. So we're at this, at least in this stage in the process, we have a measure of a hurdle rate for an investment. And let me restate the principle.
