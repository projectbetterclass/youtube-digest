# Why Half Reps Are Awesome

You've probably been told for most of your lifting career that half reps are unambiguously a bad thing, but this just isn't true. So, in this video, I'm going to give you three reasons why half reps are actually awesome and why you should implement them into your training to lead to more gains than if you were to only use full range of motion. Number one, half reps can allow you to more precisely target a muscle, specifically with compound movements. Take the bench press for example. If you use a full range of motion when you bench press, you'll train a combination of the chest and the triceps. But not all portions of the range of motion are created equal. Here's what I mean. In the bottom half of the motion, the chest will take on a brunt of the load because they're much better leveraged to push the bar up. However, in the top half of the range of motion, the chest loses a ton of its mechanical ability to push the bar and instead the triceps take over. And this is why all of the best bench pressers in the world all place a heavy emphasis on growing their triceps. In the powerlifting world, they're known as the lockout muscle. So, when you perform half reps with just the bottom portion of the range of motion, you're not really performing half reps for the chest. You're using a portion of the range where pretty much only the chest will really contribute. And this is great for not only better isolating the chest if you're having trouble doing it, but also limiting the fatigue in your triceps if you plan on isolating them with a different exercise afterward or maybe on the subsequent days. Let me give you another example of when half reps can make your reps more precise. Take the lateral raise. Many people talk about how you should perform lateral raises with your arms all the way overhead so as to achieve a full ROM raise. But in the same way that the chest and triceps don't contribute equally to all portions of a bench press, the middle delt and traps in this case don't contribute equally to all portions of a lateral raise. So there's an obvious parallel here. The middle delt is the prime mover in the bottom half of the motion while the traps take over in the top half of the motion. So you can kind of think about it like the middle delts are the chest in this context and the traps are the triceps in this context. So, by performing a full range repetition on a lateral raise, you'll be getting a heavy blend of both the middle delt and the traps. By only performing a half rep, i.e. the bottom 90°, you'll predominantly just be targeting the middle delt without the traps interfering with a ton of that stimulus. And many people report feeling a ton of traps in their lateral raises. Now, there are other reasons that you might feel your traps, which we won't be talking about in this video, but hopefully the example there is clear. Reason number two, now this one is going to sound a little bit weird, but stick with me. Half reps, ironically, allow you to challenge the entire range of a joint, whereas full range reps usually don't. So, here's an example. Imagine you're doing a normal triceps rope push down. When you get to the end of a set and you start to fatigue, what happens? Well, first the reps start to slow down, but then you start to lose an ability to fully straighten your elbow. Why? bell because we're much weaker in the elbow locked position as compared to the elbow bent position. So, every time you do a full range set of rope triceps extensions, you're challenging the bottom half of the rep significantly, but unless you dig deeply into half reps afterward to partial reps when you just continue to go as long as you can, you're never going to fully fatigue the elbow bent position in the same way that you would if you started with half reps. And even if you do perform half reps after you fully fatigue at the contracted position, on average, you still spend a lot less time challenging the more stretch position if you never solely perform half reps. When you perform half reps instead, you can dramatically increase the load and the challenge to the stretch position of the motion, which is complimentary to the full range style where you're mostly challenging the bottom. So in essence, the full range rep really mostly challenges the lockout even if you do partials afterward. and the half wrap using more weight will allow you to target more of the stretch position, which again is complimentary to the full range version. Another great example of this is really any cable pulling exercise. On any cable pulling motion, the bottom or squeeze position is much more challenging than the top or the stretch position. So, if you never perform half reps on pull downs, you're just going to be chronically underchallenging that top stretch portion of the range, which may be leaving gains on the table. So instead, you can just do some of your sets full range and then some of your sets as half reps. What you'll notice is that you can use a lot more weight for all of your half reps to adequately challenge that portion of the range. And you'll start to realize just how much that portion of the range was underloaded during your full range reps. So again, in this weird sort of irony, using half reps is one of the only ways to really actually challenge the full range of the muscle cuz that's what we are really concerned about is the muscle. Last but not least, number three. This one is super underrated, but it might actually be the most important for most people, which is that half reps are generally easier to maintain the same technique with. Why? Well, it's obvious. You're just using less range, so you have less total range of motion to have to control. As long as you're not using a stupid amount of load that's inappropriate for your strength, moving something less total distance is easier than moving something more total distance. So, if you're struggling with an exercise in terms of its execution and with your control, using a half rep style and really mastering control over a smaller portion of the range of motion is a great way to work up to a full range alternative if that's what you want to do. I cannot tell you the number of times that I have fixed someone's execution just by getting them to start with smaller ranges of motion and then gradually expanding that control over time. Once someone has an understanding of what control even feels like, then they can typically expand the range pretty easily. Not to mention the fact that a lot of times half reps just feel good because you're keeping tension on the target tissue like we talked about, especially with the bench press example on the chest or the sort of half rep variation of the triceps rope extension. Now, keep in mind that none of the reasons that I gave mean that full range reps are inherently bad. They just have different trade-offs and benefits. This is really just me pointing out the upsides to using half reps that many people still don't seem to really understand or apply to their training. So ultimately, half reps are really just another great tool to add to your lifting toolbox. And the takeaway here is don't just assume that full range is always better because there are many cases in which applying a half rep technique can actually be the more appropriate choice.
