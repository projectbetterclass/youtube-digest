# Nick Walker doesn't train hard

<https://www.youtube.com/shorts/Z98Fhb3roK8>

Nick Walker does not train very hard, and yet he is one of the best bodybuilders in the world, undoubtedly. And I mean this in a very technical failure training sense. I want you to watch the first and last reps of this set of curls that Nick has done. I'm just going to play the first and the last on repeat here. And I want you to notice how there's almost no discernible difference in terms of the rep velocity between the first and the last reps. Given that there is no drop in the velocity of these reps, he's relatively still far away from failure. This is how Nick does pretty much all of his sets. If you watch this first and last rep of a pull-down, you see something very similar. And this is true across pretty much all of his exercises, at least that I've seen. If you think this is failure training, it's not. What failure training actually looks like in general is one of two things. Either the range of motion between the first and last reps decreases, like you're seeing here between the first and the last rep of a set of dumbbell curls that I did, or when you change the resistance profile relative to the muscle strength, you'll basically go from full range to zero range, but the rep speed will be very, very grindy and slow. If you are a non-enhanced, non-super-genetically-gifted person, this is what you have to probably learn how to do at some point. Because even if you train a few reps shy of failure, which can be perfectly fine for growth, you actually need to know where failure is to be able to do that in the first place. This is one of the many reasons you probably shouldn't just copy and paste the training that you see in your favorite bodybuilders. And in fact, not training to failure for guys who are this big may actually be an adaptive feature of their programming that makes it work for them.
