Kathy Wood says the.com bubble failed because the technology wasn't ready, but today the infrastructure that was missing then is already built and working. Kathy points out that during the late '90s, critical technologies didn't exist yet. The cloud didn't arrive until 2006. Deep learning wasn't viable until 2012, and transformer architecture that enables generative AI only emerged in 2017. Kathy is making an argument most ignore. The dotcom crash wasn't proof that internet technology was overhyped. It was proof that the timing was wrong. Investors in the late 90s were funding companies that needed infrastructure that didn't exist yet. E-commerce companies needed broadband penetration that wouldn't happen for years. Streaming services needed bandwidth and compression that weren't available. Social networks needed mobile devices that hadn't been invented. The vision was right, but the execution was impossible because the foundational pieces weren't in place. So investors threw money at ideas that could generate returns for a decade. That's textbook bubble behavior, paying today for value that might arrive someday if everything goes right. Most of those bets failed because the technology couldn't deliver what was promised. Today is completely different because all the hard infrastructure problems are solved. Cloud computing exists and scales infinitely. Mobile devices reach billions of people. Bandwidth is abundant. Payment systems work globally. The foundation that the dotcom era needed is now mature and proven. AI companies aren't betting on future infrastructure. They're building on infrastructure that already works. This is highlighted when Kathy talked about founders leaving companies to join Meta's AI team. She has identified something crucial. In the com era, talent stayed scattered across hundreds of speculative ventures because no one knew what would work. Today, talent is concentrating at companies with clear advantages. Founders don't abandon their companies to join a competitor unless they see that competitor has overwhelming resources advantage that can't be matched. The migration of talent data to Meta, Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic signals those companies have pulled ahead decisively. Kathy also understands that retail investors being active isn't automatically a bubble sign. Yes, retail can drive irrational exuberance, but retail also has better access to information and tools than ever before. The difference between institutional and retail sophistication has narrowed significantly. Retail investors can access a lot of the same research data and analysis that professionals use. That doesn't eliminate stupidity. And trust me, there's plenty of that on the institutional side as well, but it means retail participation alone doesn't indicate mania. The key distinction is whether the technology delivers value now or promises it later. Companies couldn't deliver because the infrastructure didn't exist. AI companies are delivering today because everything they need already exists. That's her first point. Now, when asked about market corrections, Cathy's answer reveals how she's thinking about the next phase. Kathy predicts a correction could happen if the AI productivity boom materializes and forces the Fed to reverse course on rate cuts, catching investors offg guard. Kathy acknowledges corrections are possible and sees a specific trigger next year. The Fed just cut rates for the second time this year amid labor market weakness. Right now, everyone expects rates to keep dropping or stay low. Markets are pricing in potential future cuts through 2026. The Fed is cutting because labor markets are weakening and they're worried about growth slowing. But Kathy is arguing the AI productivity gains will flip that dynamic completely. If AI delivers the efficiency improvements she expects, economic activity will accelerate dramatically. Companies will grow faster, profits will expand, investment will surge, the level of growth would force the Fed to reverse course and raise rates to prevent the economy from overheating. Investors would get whiplashed. They're positioned for falling rates and steady growth. A sudden shift to rising rates would trigger selling as everyone reprices. That's the potential correction Kathy sees coming, not from AI failing, but from AI succeeding so dramatically that it changes the entire macro environment. This thinking is what separates Kathy from typical tech bulls. She's not just saying AI will be great. She's mapping out the second order effects. Massive productivity gains don't happen in a vacuum. They reshape labor markets, investment flows, and monetary policy. Most haven't thought through all of those implications. But Cathy also highlights 2017 and 2018 when rates rose gradually and innovation stocks still performed well. The difference was why rates were rising. In 2022, rates spiked because the Fed panicked about inflation. The increases were massive and sudden. Innovation stocks got destroyed not because rising rates always hurt growth, but because the rate shock was so severe and unexpected. When rates rise gradually in response to strong economic fundamentals, companies can handle it. The companies with real AIdriven productivity gains will recover quickly once investors understand the dynamics. The ones riding momentum without substance will struggle. The irony is that a correction triggered by too much success would actually validate Wood's entire thesis. It would prove AI is delivering transformation productivity gains, not just hype. That's very different from a bubble popping because the technology disappointed.
