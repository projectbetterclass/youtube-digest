§Primary share count: The primary share count by looking at the shares in 
the company, across all classes of shares, that are owned by public market 
investors. That information is usually embedded in the balance sheet or in 
the footnotes, but with different numbers usually for authorized, issued and 
outstanding shares. 
§Restricted shares: Restricted shares comprised shares that have been 
issued by the company to its employees, though in rare cases, they can be 
issued to the public. The restrictions take two forms. With employee 
issuances, there can be a vesting requirement, where an employee has to 
work for a specified period to be able to lay claim to the shares. The other 
is that even after vesting, these shares sometimes can come with 
restrictions on liquidity; i.e., the holders of these shares cannot trade the 
shares easily.
§Options: The third claim on equity can come from options granted by the 
company to employees and sometimes lenders, with the latter manifesting 
in convertible bonds or preferred stock. The former are part of employee 
compensation and come withspecified maturities, and often require 
vesting, just like restricted stock. The latter are included with bonds, and 
used to raise money from investors, with the conversion option usually 
running for life time of the bond, giving holders of the bond the right to 
swap their bond for shares in the company.
4
§Options give you the right to buy shares in the company at a 
fixed price (the exercise price), and as a consequence, they are 
different from primary and restricted shares in two ways. 
§The first is that if the company's stock price drops below the 
exercise price (making it out of the money), it does not make 
sense to exercise the option, thus keeping it out of the share count. 
§The second is that even when the stock price is higher than the 
exercise price (making it in-the-money), holders may not 
exercise the option, because there may be an advantage to 
holding the option for longer, hoping for a bigger payoff. This 
creates a time premium in the option, where the option is worth 
more than what you would get from exercise today. When you use an 
option pricing model, you are in effect trying to incorporate the 
current stock price, the exercise price, the likelihood that you will 
exercise the option and the expectations of future stock prices into 
a number.
5
§For some older and more mature companies, it is possible that the 
only shares outstanding are the primary shares, and getting a 
share count is easy. 
§For most companies, though, and especially yoing firms that use 
stock-based compensation heavily (because they don't have the 
cash to pay employees), the restricted shares and options 
outstanding can make a significant difference. 
§The first is just the primary share count, which will be the lowest of 
the share count numbers. 
§The second is the fully diluted share count, where you count not just 
shares outstanding today, but all shares that could be outstanding if the 
restricted stock is vested and all options (employee and conversion) get 
exercised, giving you the highest share count. 
§There is an intermediate solution of partially diluted shares, where 
rather that count all restricted stock and options, you may count only the 
vested shares (for restricted shares) and the in-the-money options (for 
employee options).
6
§The primary shares approach, with the lowest share count, 
will yield the highest value per share, but in companies with 
restricted stocks and options outstanding, that value per share 
will be too high.
§Primary share value per share = Aggregate Equity Value/ Primary 
shares outstanding
§The fully diluted shares approach, with the highest share 
count, will yield the lowest value per share, and many investors 
prefer it, contending that it is better to be conservative.
§Fully diluted share value per share = Aggregate Equity Value/ Fully 
diluted shares outstanding
§The problem with this approach, though, is that it counts all options, 
in and out of the money, as part of the share count, and not only 
assumes immediate exercise, but that the company will not receive 
the exercise proceeds, leading to an even greater underestimation.
7
§The second approach (called the Treasury stock approach) 
tries to remedy the failure to include exercise proceeds from 
option exercise in value per share, by adding those proceeds 
to the value of equity, and doing so will push your value per 
share, relative to a diluted share value:
§Treasury stock value per share = [Aggregate value of equity + 
Strike Price × Options outstanding]/ Partially diluted shares 
outstanding
§Here, you use only the options that are in the money, 
because out-of-the-money will give you a value boost by 
bringing an exercise price higher than the stock price that is 
fantasy . 
§The problem with this approach is that it ignores the time 
premium on options, effectively undervaluing those 
options, and will result in a value per share that is too high.
8
§The right approach in valuation is to estimate the value of 
options, using an option pricing model) and then subtracting 
that value from aggregate equity value to arrive at a equity 
value for just shares outstanding (primary and treasury). 
Dividing that residual value by a share count that includes only 
primary and restricted shares will come closest to capturing 
the correct value per share:
§Option-adjusted value per share = [Aggregate value ofequity− 
Value of options outstanding]/ (Primary + Restricted Shares 
outstanding)
§With this approach, you no longer have to separate in-the-
money from out-of-the-money options, since both will have 
value, and the option pricing model will adjust the value for the 
exercise price (relative to stock price) and the life left on the 
option.

§ Note that I have studiously avoided trying to estimate the value per 
share, by share class, in spite of the fact that the shares differ in voting 
rights.
§ That is because of the company's uncommon voting structure, where 
the voting rights reside in a single share owned by the founders. 
§The closest parallel I can think of for this structure are the golden shares 
that governments put into government-owned utilities and companies 
that were privatized in the 1980s and 1990s. That golden share gives 
governments veto power over major decisions, but not power over the 
day-to-day operating choices made by a company. 
§Anthropic's founder share is far more powerful, and I do think that there 
is a danger in loading up this much power in a single share. I don't 
want to give governments and regulators ideas, but I can visualize an 
activist regulator, finding Anthropic at fault for an AI accident, and 
deciding to expropriate the founder's share to make the company a 
government-controlled utility. I am sure that the founders will try to get the 
courts to step in, claiming (rightly) expropriation, but why tempt fate?

§Yo u  may be uninterested in intrinsic value and focused on 
pricing Anthropic in the market, but you will still run into 
share count in multiple places. 
§The first is in the market cap, a number that gets used widely to 
rank firms and talk about valuations, where using different share 
count measures can lead to very different market cap estimates.
§ The second is in computing pricing multiples, where market 
cap becomes an input into estimating enterprise value or is 
used a stand alone. 
§The third is in computing per share numbers for earnings, 
revenues or any other metric, since per share requires the use of a 
share count; the primary earnings per share will be very different 
from fully diluted earnings per share.

§What is the correct market capitalization for a company? I can

around to get a market capitalization:
§Option-adjusted Market Capitalization = (Share price× Number of 
primary and restricted shares outstanding) + V alue of employee

§This will yield values that allow you to compare companies
