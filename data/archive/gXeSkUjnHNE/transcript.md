What's the difference between these three types of lateral raise variations? Number one, where you're leaning away from something. Number two, where you're just standing upright like a normal raise. And number three, where you're actually leaning into something. Well, the differences ultimately come down to just understanding where each exercise is hardest and what that does for muscle recruitment in all three cases. So, starting with the leaning variation, this exercise is hardest when the arm is roughly parallel to the ground. This is because what is called the moment arm is longest in this position, which means there's the highest amount of resistance on the shoulder. So the leaning lateral raise is hardest when your arm is roughly here in this position. In other words, above 90° from the ground getting closer to the overhead position. Now compare that to the normal dumbbell lateral raise where the exercise is hardest when the arm is roughly 90° away from the side. Again, this is just because of moment arm distance where the dumbbell is farthest from the shoulder. Lastly, the leaning in variation is hardest when your arm is again parallel to the floor. But because you're leaning now, your arm is actually loaded most below 90°. The leaning variation is heaviest here. The normal variation is heaviest here. And the leaning in variation is heaviest here. And if we think about muscle recruitment here on a spectrum of most middle delt to most other muscles, the leaning in variation will include probably the most amount of middle delt recruitment is because in the bottom half of any raise, the middle delt is proportionately more active to the other muscles that control the shoulder blade like the traps and the serrus anterior. In other words, if you load the bottom a little bit more, the middle delt will take on more load as compared to those other shoulder movers. On the flip side, if you perform the leaning variation where the load is heaviest with your arm above 90°, that will put proportionately more challenge on the other shoulder muscles again like the traps and the serrus anterior. This doesn't necessarily mean that these muscles will be the rate limiter of the motion, but they'll be proportionately a lot more involved compared to the leaning away variation. And then the traditional raise with the arms loaded most around 90 degrees is going to be a kind of blend of the two where you're going to have a great amount of middle delt recruitment, but you're also going to have a great amount of those other shoulder stabilizers involved. If your goals are to train the middle deltically, I would choose the variation where you're leaning away from the working side arm where the dumbbell can be heaviest when your arm is below parallel to the ground. If you want a much greater proportion of the traps and the serrus involved, then you're going to want to do the leaning away variation where your arm is loaded most when the arm is above 90° from the body. And then if you kind of want to blend those two goals together, pick the normal dumbbell lateral raise where you get a great amount of involvement of both the middle delts and the other shoulder movers.
