# There are valid reasons for someone to use higher rep ranges

Stop wondering why you're not making any progress in the gym when you're training like a [ __ ] doing 10, 12, 20 reps on weight training exercises. This is not cardio. If you're really trying to grow a muscle and see progress, stick to the 6 to 8 rep range rule. You should be failing between the 6 to 8th rep, maybe 10, but over 10 is just like up the weight. Like if you don't like push yourself, oh my gosh. Increase the weight. If you can do that many reps and you're still not failing, you are not pushing yourself. And of course, when I say 6 to 8, I mean 6 to 8 with good form. >> [sighs] >> Guys, there's nothing wrong with doing more than 10 reps. If anything, I think it's funny because technically doing higher reps is harder. It feels more difficult, which is part of the reason that using a more moderate rep range can be more beneficial for people since it may be easier for them to push to failure and not fail first due to things like afferent feedback or, you know, your cardio. When people say stuff like this, it just shows me that they don't have that much experience with programming or likely haven't coached very many people before because there are reasons to use higher rep ranges. For example, if an exercise doesn't have the best bracing and an individual is rather strong, let's say the standing chest cable fly, it might make more sense to do higher reps. That way they don't have to brace quite as hard and feel like they're being pulled back by the cables. It wouldn't make sense for them to do four to seven reps, and instead they might want to do eight to 12 reps. Maybe somebody has more joint discomfort when they're using more moderate rep ranges and want to do eight to 12 or even up to 15 reps. Again, nothing wrong with that. And I'm saying this is somebody who programs mostly between 6 to 10 reps. So, if you like your higher rep ranges, don't worry, you can still make good progress so long as you're training close to or to failure.
