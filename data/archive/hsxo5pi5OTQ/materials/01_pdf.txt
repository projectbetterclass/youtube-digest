§Chronological age: The first and simplest choice is to look at 
the age of a business, putting companies that have been in 
operation for short periods into the young company grouping 
and classifying those that have been in operation for longer as 
mature. 
§Industry grouping: Another choice is categorizing industry 
groups into life cycle stages and assuming that any company 
that belongs to a specific group is in the same stage. For 
decades, investors have viewed technology as a shorthand for 
growth and put technology companies into the earlier stages of 
the life cycle and utilities as mature or even declining and put 
all utility companies into the same life cycle stage.

§Financial statements: The current financial statements of a 
company provide information on unit economics, 
profitability and risk, and historical financial statements are 
useful in assessing growth in the business and changes in 
business profitability. 
§Market size and growth: Knowing the size of the total market 
that a company is aiming for, and how quickly it is growing 
can help put reasonability constraints on the growth rate and 
market you estimate for a company. 
§Industry statistics: As you forecast profit margins and the risk 
profile for a company, having that information for the cross 
section of companies in the industry can establish bounds 
and help in making estimates.

§If you are pricing a company in a business, where there are 
lots of publicly traded companies, you will not only have a 
choice of pricing multiples, but also more robust pricing, since 
each of the companies has an updated market price and the 
financial statements are standardized (GAAP or IFRS).
§If you are pricing a company in a business, where there lots 
of companies that are privately held, as is often the case 
when you have a nascent business with lots of potential, you are 
confronting a challenge that many venture capitalists face, and 
you can try to get the most recent pricing for each company, 
though that pricing will often be dated (depending on when 
the last capital raise was) and sometimes untrustworthy.
§Yo u r  most difficult challenge will be when you are pricing a 
company in a business, where you have both very few 
companies in the space, and almost none are publicly traded.

§Yo u  can price a company based upon any metric that you 
choose to use, assuming that it is viable, but if pressed on 
which metric is the best one, I will deflect (and perhaps deliver 
a non-response), because I don't believe that there is a 
"best" metric for pricing. 
§The key, though, is that no matter what metric you pick, you 
should think through how that metric will play out in the 
company's value and what you need to control for in your 
pricing.
§And since pricing is statistics, first and foremost, you want to 
choose a metric where you can get more firms in your peer 
group.

§As you can see, for Anthropic to be highly priced relative to revenue, its 
revenues will have to not only grow a lot but do so quickly, making the 
revenue growth rate and the speed at which it ramps up the first 
determinants. 
§The second component is profitability, since the company is reporting 
sizable losses now, and can expect to do so in the near term. Here, there 
are two key determinants that will drive value, with the first being unit 
economics, since having higher gross margins will ease the pathway from 
losses to profits, and the second being economies of scale, with compute 
costs growing slower than revenues, pushing up operating margins. 
§The third is risk, with operating risk (from evolving business models), 
financial risk (from the compute commitments) and regulatory risk (from 
restrictions on AI) all coming into play; companies that are less exposed to 
these risks should be worth more. T
§o make a case for Anthropic at a $1.5 or $2 trillion pricing, you will have to 
make defensible arguments for sky-high revenue growth in the early 
year, a surge in operating margins to 30% or higher, with favorable 
unit economics and economies of scale providing tailwinds and a risk 
profile that quickly converges towards the average, as regulatory 
risks recede and the business model finds it feet.

§As Anthropic heads towards its initial public offering, my suggestion is that 
you step back from the frenzy, since like the parable of the six blind men 
who visit an elephant, and having touched different parts of it, come away 
with wildly different descriptions of the animal, there will be Anthropic 
bulls and bears who will latch on to a part of the company's lore and spin 
tales about its future. 
§For bulls, that story is going to be anchored in a belief that AI's reach in terms of 
market will be massive, that Anthropic will be one of the big winners in that 
market and that it will find a way to convert its dominance into market share and 
high profits; the pricing will be tied to high revenue growth and great unit 
economics (with high gross margins). 
§For bears, the story will be premised on an AI market that struggles to get off 
the ground, and Anthropic, as a company, being constrained by weak 
corporate governance and an untrustworthy management. Their pricing 
argument will point to the revenue growth that will be lower than anticipated, 
which combined with their compute commitments will lead to profit margin 
compression, making the nosebleed multiples of revenues that you will be 
paying for the company an overpricing. 
§I will not be taking sides here, but not because I am copping out. It is 
because I am investor at heart and feel more comfortable making my 
decision based upon an intrinsic valuation of Anthropic, with all its 
flaws and uncertainties, which will have to wait until the full prospectus 
gets filed.
