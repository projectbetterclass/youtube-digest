There are three main reasons that you feel like your traps are on fire whenever you're doing lateral raises. The first is how you've set up the forces of the exercise. The second is the range of motion you're using whenever you perform raises. And the third is the intent or the cues that you're using when you raise. Let's address all three of these. All right, so the number one reason is just simply how you've set up the forces. Think about a traditional dumbbell lateral raise and ask yourself, what are the dumbbells trying to do to my arms the entire time? Right from the start, the dumbbells are pulling your arms directly downward toward the floor. And what muscle groups move your shoulders upward toward the ceiling? You guessed it, the traps. So, if your traps are on fire whenever you raise, it's not because there's a problem with you. It's actually because your traps are functioning appropriately. So, what you need to do is change the direction that the force is pulling your arm first right from the start of the movement. And the easiest way to do this is just to use a cable that pulls your arm across your body like this. So now instead of pulling downward, the cable simply tries to rotate your arm across your body, which only the middle delts can actually resist. And if you don't have a cable, you can simply just apply the same exact concept to a dumbbell raise by using one arm at a time. But instead of sanding, simply just lie sideways on a low incline bench. And now what the dumbbell does is it smooshes your arm into your body, just like the cable lateral raise we looked at. Now, even if you alter the force so that the middle delts are less automatically recruited than the traps, if you don't get these next two things right, you still might feel like your traps are dominating the movement. So, that leads us into reason number two, which is the range of motion that you're using. Raising your arm all the way overhead involves movement of bones that are not just the ball and socket of the shoulder. Because at a certain point in raising, your shoulder blade and your collar bone need to rotate upward toward the ceiling. This is known as upward rotation and elevation of the shoulder blade, which the middle and upper traps are responsible for doing. But here's where this fix comes in. The shoulder blade doesn't actually move very much when your arm is raised in this lower part of the motion because it doesn't need to. So if you want to maximize middle delt involvement while minimizing trap recruitment, just use less range of motion. Now, this may seem like a bad thing, but it's actually a good thing if your goals are just really to isolate for the middle delt. Now, guess what? Even if you're doing both of those first two things perfectly, this third thing can actually screw up both of those. Which leads me to reason or change number three, which is how you think about or cue yourself to perform the motion. Instead of thinking about raising upward, think about raising outward, almost like you're trying to touch the walls of the gym to either side of you. Using an intent to raise your arm outward will prevent you from unconsciously shrugging upward when you initiate the motion. And with these three changes in mind, you should pretty much only feel your middle delts and nothing else.
