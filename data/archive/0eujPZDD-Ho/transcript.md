Each of these technological transformations involved massive capital investment. Many failed companies but ultimately delivered enormous value. The automotive industry's early days saw hundreds of car manufacturers, most of which failed. But the survivors transformed transportation and enabled suburban development, interstate commerce, and modern logistics. Television required massive investment in broadcast infrastructure, content production, and receiver manufacturing with many failures along the way, but created entirely new industries changed how society consumed information and entertainment. The internet bubble is the most directly relevant parallel because it's the most recent and involves many of the same investors and companies now funding AI. Jaime mentions that during the bubble, there were 100 companies worth $50 billion and they disappeared. Investors who brought these companies at peak valuations lost everything. Yet Jaime points out the value that came from Facebook, YouTube, and Google. Companies that created trillions in value and fundamentally transformed how people communicate, find information, and consume entertainment. The societal and economic value created by the survivors vastly exceeded the capital destroyed in failures. From society's perspective, the internet bubble was hugely productive despite being terrible for many individual investors. The overinvestment built fiber optic networks and enabled high-speed internet. Created distribution infrastructure and enabled e-commerce, trained a generation of engineers and entrepreneurs in internet technologies and funded experimentation that identified which business models would work. When the bubble burst and companies failed, the physical infrastructure and human capital remained and became the foundation for the next generation of successful companies. Google bought cheap server capacity from failed startups. Facebook recruited engineers who had learned at failed.com companies. Amazon AWS utilized excess capacity from overbuilt data centers. The parallel to AI suggests a similar pattern could unfold. There will be massive overinvestment. Many AI companies will fail. Investors in those companies will lose money. But the infrastructure built and knowledge develops will enable the successful companies to create enormous value. Open AI or anthropic might fail, but their research and infrastructure will inform whatever succeeds them. Data centers being built may have lower utilization than projected, but they'll exist and be repurposed as applications develop. Engineers trained on AI projects will carry that knowledge to future ventures. The historical pattern is that most investments in transformational technologies fail. You need to pick the winners, which is extremely difficult during the hype phase when everything looks promising. Even sophisticated investors with good information get this wrong a lot of the time. I think Jamie made a good point on timing. The internet bubble peaked in 2000 and crashed in 2001 2002. But Google went public in 2004 and Facebook in 2012 years after the bubble burst. The biggest winners emerged after the excitement died down when practical business models were being built on the infrastructure created during the bubble. They might not be the high-profile startups, but companies founded 3 to 5 years from now that build practical applications on the infrastructure being created today.
