The Cortisol Awakening Response and Entrainment 
“This line represents the healthy cortisol curve. Most people don’t realise that, but you wake up 
because of a rise in cortisol. It’s literally called the cortisol awakening response. If you’ve ever 
woken up one minute before your alarm clock, it’s because the cortisol awakening response is 
heavily entrainable, meaning it can be matched to external stimuli.” 
The cortisol awakening response (CAR) is a well-established phenomenon. The cortisol 
awakening response (CAR) is the rapid increase in cortisol levels across the first 30–45 minutes 
following morning awakening. In healthy individuals, free salivary cortisol increases between 50% 
and 160% during this post-awakening window, peaking approximately 30 minutes after waking. A 
2025 Endocrine Reviews synthesis describes the CAR as a strong secretory burst at the start of the 
active phase and proposes two adaptive functions: mobilising resources for anticipated 
activity-related demands and counter-regulating adverse emotional experience from the previous 
day. 
The causal direction stated is currently not supported by the literature. Some studies found 
that awakenings during the night produce lower cortisol levels than morning awakenings, or no 
significant mean increase, which supports a strong circadian contribution. A 2025 in vivo 
microdialysis study of 201 volunteers found that long sleepers reached maximal cortisol secretion 
well before waking, while short sleepers peaked after it. But nocturnal-awakening evidence is not 
uniform. One earlier study found a cortisol increase after nocturnal awakening, although at lower 
absolute levels than morning awakening, and another protocol paper cites anticipatory ACTH rises 
when early waking is expected rather than surprising. 
CAR magnitude seems to be modulated by anticipated wake time, day of the week and 
anticipated demand, which is the anticipation hypothesis. That hypothesis has accumulated 
supportive but inconsistent evidence since the mid-1990s. Waking a minute before an alarm is better 
explained by circadian and homeostatic sleep-timing mechanisms than by cortisol specifically. 
The main limitation of the cortisol awakening response literature is that this is hard to 
measure accurately and is shaped by methodological noise: most studies rely on home saliva 
sampling, where small delays after waking, weak adherence monitoring, and inconsistent reporting 
can distort estimates. Results are also difficult to compare because studies use different operational
definitions, often conflating the true post-awakening change with the cortisol level at waking, and 
many older studies lacked the methodological controls now recommended. A second limitation is 
high biological variability: cortisol near waking varies markedly between and within individuals and is 
influenced by sleep duration, wake-time regularity, chronotype, light, stress, weekday effects, and 
physical activity, which helps explain why sleep findings are inconsistent across studies. Finally, the 
field still lacks full agreement on what the CAR represents biologically: many studies and reviews 
treat it as a relatively distinct response associated with awakening, superimposed on the circadian 
cortisol rhythm, whereas recent work suggests that much of the apparent response may reflect 
pre-awakening circadian dynamics, with the post-awakening deviation becoming non-significant 
under some non-linear models. Consequently, whether awakening itself triggers a discrete additional 
cortisol response and what functional significance that response retains remains debated.

High Morning Cortisol as a Prerequisite for Mood, Focus and 
Sleep 
“You want this peak in cortisol as high as possible in the first 60 minutes of your day. Everyone 
thinks you want low cortisol, but here’s the deal. High cortisol in the early part of your day is 
what provides for mood, elevated mood, focus, attention, your ability to learn. It’s also what 
establishes low cortisol in the afternoon and at night, which is a requirement for falling and 
staying asleep.” 
Cortisol Awakening Response versus Waking Cortisol 
Waking cortisol is the baseline level at awakening; CAR is the dynamic increase over the next 30–45 
minutes. Waking cortisol (S1) refers to the cortisol concentration measured immediately upon 
awakening, serving as the starting point of the post-sleep active phase. The CAR is considered a 
discrete phenomenon superimposed on the circadian cortisol rhythm, not just a continuation of the 
pre-awakening rise. Expert consensus guidelines emphasise that waking cortisol and the CAR must 
be measured and reported separately, as they capture distinct physiological components. The first 
sample (S1) quantifies the waking cortisol level, while subsequent samples at fixed intervals capture 
the dynamic CAR increase. The two components are often inversely related, with a lower CAR 
following a higher waking cortisol level and vice versa, consistent with the law of initial value.
Cortisol-Cognition Findings 
 The relationship between morning cortisol and cognition depends heavily on whether 
cortisol is acutely elevated or chronically dysregulated. A meta-analysis of experimental cortisol 
administration found that morning cortisol administration significantly impaired memory, while 
afternoon administration produced a small enhancement. 
In contrast to chronically elevated cortisol, a robust cortisol awakening response (CAR) may 
serve a preparatory, neuroprotective function. A robust CAR predicts less hippocampal and prefrontal 
activity but enhanced functional coupling between these regions during demanding tasks hours later, 
indicating improved neurocognitive efficiency. Pharmacological suppression of the CAR impaired 
emotional discrimination and reduced the fractional occupancy of brain states supporting emotional 
and working memory processing. In healthy adults with poor sleep quality, a blunted CAR was linked 
to worse verbal memory, visual memory, and executive function. Additionally, in adolescents with 
ADHD, lower cortisol at awakening was associated with poorer attention and memory. 
A well-regulated CAR appears to support brain preparedness and executive efficiency, while 
chronically elevated or acutely spiked cortisol tends to impair memory encoding, attention, and 
learning, with effects further moderated by sleep quality, stress context, and individual differences in 
HPA axis regulation. 
Cortisol Awakening Response Findings 
Glucocorticoid secretion around waking is described as preparing the organism for the 
demands of the coming day, and the Endocrine Reviews model links the CAR to daily activity in 
metabolic, immunological and neurocognitive systems. Evidence does not support the idea that the 
highest possible peak is the healthiest. An early review concluded it was unclear whether better 
health and positive affect are linked to larger or smaller awakening responses, and a large 
meta-analysis found mixed psychosocial associations, with larger responses linked to life stress and 
job stress but smaller responses linked to fatigue, burnout, and exhaustion. A bigger rise on one day 
can reflect anticipated challenge, an earlier circadian phase, shorter sleep plus higher prior activity, 
or other context rather than “better” physiology. Both exaggerated and blunted responses appear in 
clinical populations: chronic stress and some depressive presentations are associated with a 
heightened CAR, and burnout and post-traumatic stress with a blunted one. Better objective sleep 
efficiency and longer sleep time were associated with higher CAR the next morning in college
students. Some other studies found shorter sleep linked to greater CAR or larger CAR with 
worse-than-usual sleep, showing inconsistency. Studies that directly sampled bedtime or nocturnal 
cortisol found that higher pre-sleep cortisol predicted shorter sleep, lower sleep efficiency, and 
longer sleep latency that same night.  
Waking Cortisol Findings 
 Higher waking cortisol is a feature of a well-regulated HPA axis and is associated with better 
sleep health in several studies. In the MESA cohort, higher wakeup cortisol predicted longer sleep 
duration, higher sleep efficiency, and reduced sleep irregularity. Similarly, higher morning cortisol in 
shift workers was associated with better sleep quality, while those with lower morning cortisol had 
poorer sleep. A 15-day intensive longitudinal study found that individuals with longer average sleep 
had steeper diurnal cortisol slopes, but the study measured pre-sleep cortisol rather than waking 
cortisol as the predictor of that night's sleep. 
The evidence has not established a direct causal chain from high waking cortisol to low 
nighttime cortisol. Rather, both are components of a healthy diurnal cortisol rhythm: cortisol peaks 
after awakening and declines to a nadir around midnight. A steeper diurnal decline (higher morning, 
lower evening) is associated with better health outcomes, and individuals with longer, better-quality 
sleep exhibit steeper cortisol slopes. 
Poor sleep disrupts this rhythm. Sleep loss delays the onset of the cortisol quiescent period 
and raises evening cortisol by 37–45% the following evening, reflecting impaired negative feedback. 
Individuals with insomnia show moderately elevated cortisol overall, and those with higher 
wake-after-sleep-onset have lower awakening cortisol the next day, possibly due to temporary 
inhibition of cortisol secretion after nocturnal elevations.  
The literature supports an association between a robust cortisol awakening response and 
favourable sleep outcomes, but the evidence is insufficient to claim that higher waking cortisol 
causally produces lower nighttime cortisol or better sleep the following night. This relationship 
seems to reflect a common underlying regulation of the HPA axis.

A Flattened Cortisol Curve, Lifespan and Recovery from Illness
“If this peak is not as high, and this is kind of a, like a medium or, or flat, what they call a 
flattening of the cortisol curve, you see shorter lifespan, less recovery from every kind of illness 
and, and these stress bouts last a very long time because they’re riding on a higher baseline.” 
A blunted cortisol awakening response (CAR) reflects compromised HPA axis functioning and 
has been linked to psychopathology, burnout, and physical health vulnerability. The most direct 
mortality evidence comes from a 16-year prospective study showing that diminished cortisol 
response to mental stress predicted all-cause mortality (HR = 1.33, 95% CI 1.01–1.76), with 
approximately 13% of this risk mediated by elevated IL-6 inflammation. The mortality finding is 
notable but comes from cortisol reactivity to a laboratory stressor rather than the CAR specifically. 
Individuals with diminished cortisol responses also showed higher depressive symptoms, increased 
inflammation (IL-6), and lower exercise rates. 
A systematic review of 47 prospective studies further confirmed that blunted stress reactivity 
predicted greater illness frequency, poorer self-reported health, and physical disability. This 
systematic review by Turner et al. found that only one-third of prospective findings were significant, 
yet blunted reactivity consistently predicted distinct adverse outcomes, including obesity, 
depression, anxiety, PTSD symptoms, and musculoskeletal pain. Blunted cardiovascular and cortisol 
reactivity more broadly has been proposed as an independent pathway to ill health.

Flattened Cortisol and Insulin Insensitivity 
“Okay, so this, if we were to flatten this bump, it’s literally called a flattening of the cortisol 
curve, you start running into all sorts of problems. lnsulin insensitivity, metabolic issues, and on 
and on and on.” 
Flattening of the diurnal cortisol curve, characterised by a reduced morning peak, elevated 
evening levels, and a less steep decline across the day, has been associated with worsened 
glycaemia, insulin resistance, and incident type 2 diabetes in large cohort studies. The relationship 
between cortisol flattening and metabolic dysfunction appears bidirectional, with hyperglycemia
promoting HPA-axis dysfunction and flattened cortisol profiles further worsening glycemia in a 
cyclical pattern. 
Among participants with diabetes in MESA, a flatter overall cortisol decline slope was 
associated with a 54.7% higher HbA1c per unit increase in log cortisol, while bedtime cortisol and 
total AUC were also significantly positively associated with worsened glycemia. Longitudinal MESA 
data confirmed that among those with diabetes, each annual percent increase in wake-up cortisol, 
area under the curve (AUC), and overall decline slope was associated with significant increases in 
fasting glucose over 6 years. 
In the CARDIA study of middle-aged adults, a robust cortisol awakening response was 
associated with 53% lower odds of incident diabetes over 10 years among whites, while a flatter late 
decline slope was associated with 416% higher odds. Notably, no significant cortisol-diabetes 
associations were observed among African Americans in this cohort, suggesting racial heterogeneity 
in the cortisol-glycemia relationship. In non-diabetic hypertensive patients, daily cortisol AUC and 
dexamethasone-suppressed cortisol independently predicted impaired glucose tolerance and insulin 
resistance, indicating that even subtle cortisol dysregulation may increase diabetes risk. 
Sleep restriction in men induces insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia, and experimentally 
clamping cortisol and testosterone mitigates this insulin resistance by over 50%, identifying cortisol 
dysregulation as a causal mediator of sleep-loss-related metabolic harm.

Morning Hydration, the Ascending Vagal Pathway and Cortisol 
“There’s an ascending pathway along the vagus nerve, which is a bunch of different pathways, 
but along the vagus nerve, that serves to wake up the brain, and part of that has to do with 
hydration first thing in the morning… waking up and hydrating first thing in the morning is 
immensely beneficial for mental vigor, physical vigor. It provides a small but meaningful 
increase in cortisol at this time.”
Mechanism of Vagal Activation 
Drinking water reliably stimulates cardiac vagal activity within 20–30 minutes of ingestion, as 
demonstrated across multiple controlled studies measuring heart rate variability. However, no study 
in the available literature directly tests whether morning water ingestion augments the cortisol 
awakening response, and the two physiological domains (vagal activation and CAR) have not been 
experimentally linked through a water-ingestion paradigm.  
Water ingestion produces a coordinated autonomic response: sympathetic vasoconstrictor 
activity rises while cardiac vagal tone increases in parallel, preventing blood pressure elevation in 
healthy individuals. Spectral markers of vagal tone increase with the onset of drinking and remain 
elevated for up to 60 minutes. Cold water appears to produce a more rapid vagal response, with Root 
Mean Square of Successive Differences (rMSSD, a metric used to measure heart rate variability) 
increasing immediately upon ingestion. Hydration also accelerates post-exercise vagal reactivation, 
supporting a broader role for fluid intake in parasympathetic regulation.  
Cortisol Awakening Response and Water Intake 
 The cortisol awakening response describes a cortisol increase within 30–45 minutes of 
waking, likely regulated by circadian, neurocognitive, and environmental inputs. No study found 
during this search directly examines whether water ingestion upon waking modulates the CAR. One 
study (Zaplatosch et al., 2025) in young healthy males found no association between habitual fluid 
intake, hydration status, and any cortisol dynamic, including the CAR, even when controlling for 
physical activity, sleep, and body fat percentage. An earlier trial (Mericq and Cutler, 1998) found that 
high fluid intake (5 L/day) increased urinary free cortisol excretion, though the result likely reflected 
reduced renal reabsorption, not elevated cortisol production. 
The temporal profile further complicates a causal link: vagal activation peaks 20–25 minutes 
after water ingestion, while salivary cortisol begins rising approximately 10 minutes post-awakening. 
A recent study using ambulatory microdialysis found no change in cortisol secretion rate after waking 
compared with the pre-waking hour, questioning whether awakening itself (let alone water 
ingestion) triggers cortisol release.
Connecting Vagal Activity, Brain Arousal, and Cortisol 
● Dehydration reduces brain activation in orbitofrontal cortex, cingulate gyrus, hypothalamus, 
and amygdala, with these deactivations linked to reduced HRV and greater perceived effort.  
● Vagal-cardiac changes correlate with brain activity: HRV indices (SDNN, RMSSD) significantly 
correlate with BOLD signal in the medial orbitofrontal cortex and ventral cingulate gyrus. 
● CAR and brain preparedness: A robust CAR predicts enhanced hippocampal-prefrontal 
coupling and improved working memory efficiency hours later.  
● ANS–HPA interaction: One clinical study found that combined parasympathetic activity and 
CAR magnitude explained greater variance in perceived stress than either system alone.  
The evidence thus supports two separate but parallel chains: water ingestion activates vagal 
pathways and hydration status influences brain arousal networks, while the CAR independently 
prepares the brain for daily cognitive demands. Whether water ingestion upon waking bridges these 
chains to produce a measurable CAR increase remains empirically untested, and the null findings 
from hydration–cortisol studies suggest any such effect, if present, is likely small.

Melanopsin Ganglion Cells and the Suprachiasmatic Nucleus 
“When you view sunlight… it activates these melanopsin intrinsically photosensitive ganglion 
cells… They literally extend physical wires that we call axons into your hypothalamus. Your 
master circadian clock resides there. We call that your suprachiasmatic nucleus.” 
Sunlight activates a specialised subset of retinal ganglion cells (ipRGC) that express the 
photopigment melanopsin, which are intrinsically photosensitive and depolarise in response to light 
via a G-protein-coupled cascade. Light triggers melanopsin's G-protein cascade, causing membrane 
depolarisation and sustained action-potential firing, a response that is opposite to rod/cone 
hyperpolarisation and well-suited for irradiance integration over long durations. M1-type ipRGCs are 
the principal subtype projecting to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), which serves as the principal 
circadian clock in mammals. These ipRGCs co-release glutamate and PACAP onto SCN neurones,
providing the neurochemical signals that reset the molecular clock. Rod and cone photoreceptors are 
dispensable for photoentrainment; mice lacking both still entrain to light-dark cycles.

The “Optimal Stimulus” for the Circadian Clock 
“The specific wavelengths of light, as we call them, that are coming from the sun when it’s low 
in the sky are the optimal stimulus for these melanopsin intrinsically photosensitive cells that 
tell your central clock it’s time to wake up.” 
The circadian system is strongly regulated by environmental light, with light received in the 
biological morning generally tending to advance the circadian clock and light in the biological evening 
tending to delay it. These signals reach the brain’s master circadian clock, the suprachiasmatic 
nucleus (SCN), through intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs). These cells contain 
melanopsin, a photopigment with peak sensitivity at approximately 480 nm, but they also receive 
input from rods and cones. Cone photoreceptors contribute substantially to circadian responses at 
the onset of light exposure and at moderate irradiances, while melanopsin dominates during 
sustained, high-irradiance exposures. Interestingly, melanopsin-mediated circadian responses can 
persist even in rare individuals without functional rods and cones, demonstrating that this pathway 
can independently detect environmental light.  
However, there is no compelling evidence that the particular wavelengths present when the 
sun is low above the horizon constitute an “optimal stimulus” for these cells. In fact, direct sunlight 
becomes relatively depleted in shorter blue wavelengths when the sun is low because those 
wavelengths are preferentially scattered by the atmosphere. The ambient sky during twilight, 
particularly when the sun is below the horizon, does undergo systematic spectral changes and can 
become relatively enriched in shorter wavelengths, but this is different from saying that low-angle 
sunlight itself has a uniquely optimal melanopsin spectrum. 
There is nevertheless compelling evidence that twilight contains useful circadian information 
beyond simple brightness. In mice, researchers have shown that the blue–yellow colour changes 
accompanying twilight can influence neurons in the SCN. When cone-defined colour was varied 
independently of rod and melanopsin stimulation, SCN neurons responded to those colour changes,
and mice exposed to simulated natural changes in twilight colour showed better alignment of their 
circadian rhythms with the solar cycle than mice exposed to changes in brightness alone. This 
demonstrates that, in mice, cone-derived colour information can supplement irradiance as a signal of 
solar position. 
Whether humans use twilight colour in the same way remains uncertain. A human 
experiment that manipulated blue–yellow colour while keeping melanopsin stimulation constant 
found no conclusive effects on circadian phase, melatonin suppression, alertness or sleep. Human 
experiments do show that rods and cones can contribute to circadian light responses, particularly 
early in an exposure or under some lower-light conditions, while melanopsin tends to become 
increasingly dominant during prolonged exposure. Other studies, however, have found little or no 
detectable cone contribution under specific conditions. 
Across human studies as a whole, melanopic illuminance, a measure of how strongly a light 
source stimulates the melanopsin system, remains the best single predictor of circadian responses to 
different spectra of light. But intensity, exposure duration, circadian timing, prior light exposure and 
contributions from rods and cones all influence the eventual response. 
Overall, morning light strongly engages the ipRGC–SCN circadian pathway, and the spectral 
composition of natural light can matter. But the assertion that the specific wavelengths produced 
when the sun is low in the sky are the “optimal stimulus” for melanopsin cells is not established. The 
benefit of morning outdoor light is better supported by its timing, high overall and melanopic 
illumination, and its role in circadian phase resetting. 
Limitations 
 The most obvious gap is that human mechanistic evidence is thinner than the mouse and 
retinal-circuit evidence. Human studies mostly infer circuitry from melatonin suppression or phase 
shifts under controlled nighttime exposures, while direct study of human ipRGCs has been rare. 
 A second gap is that real-world twilight exposure is poorly represented. Multiple reviews 
argue that laboratory studies use simplified, static light exposures, while actual dawn and dusk vary 
in intensity, spectrum, timing, light history, and individual behaviour. This gap persists because 
dynamic naturalistic exposure is technically hard to quantify and compare and because there is still
no universally agreed replacement for conventional photometric measures across all non-visual 
responses. 
A major limitation is that many studies cannot cleanly separate melanopsin from cone and 
rod input. The photoreceptor sensitivity curves overlap, most older studies relied on monochromatic 
light rather than silent substitution, and even integrated models still leave ambiguity about which 
photoreceptor dominates under which duration and irradiance. That helps explain why the S-cone 
story remains contested. 
The cellular mechanism is also incomplete. Much of the mechanistic work comes from 
M1-biased or mouse-heavy datasets, and the field still lacks a full map of human ipRGC subtype 
functions, projections, and signalling diversity. 
The main limitation is not that the studies disagree about melanopsin being central. It is that 
they still do not fully resolve how multiple photoreceptors, diverse ipRGC subtypes, and dynamic 
real-world light jointly determine human circadian entrainment.

Morning Sunlight and Cortisol 
“In the first hour of your day, there’s a unique window that passes after about an hour 
following waking, where your suprachiasmatic clock can trigger an alternate pathway that 
literally goes down your spinal column and triggers your adrenals to release additional cortisol. 
In fact, viewing sunlight in the first hour of your day takes this peak.” 
Studies confirm that bright light in the early morning elevates cortisol above dim-light 
conditions, with effect sizes ranging from approximately 35% to over 50%. The cortisol awakening 
response (CAR), a sharp cortisol rise within 30–45 minutes of waking, occurs even in complete 
darkness, but light exposure amplifies this endogenous surge. 
A 2005 Cell Metabolism study showed that light induces gene expression in the adrenal 
gland via the suprachiasmatic nucleus and the sympathetic nervous system, and that this is 
accompanied by a surge in plasma and brain corticosterone without activation of the 
hypothalamic-pituitary axis. The mechanism was confirmed by two independent manipulations: SCN
lesioning abolished the effect, and adrenal denervation blocked light-induced corticosterone release. 
Earlier work had already shown that light enhances sympathetic and suppresses vagal outflow and 
that SCN lesions eliminate the effect. 
Two limitations need stating. First, this is rodent work, performed in mice and rats, some of it 
under urethane anaesthesia. It has not been demonstrated in humans, where the ethical and 
technical barriers to adrenal denervation are unambiguous. Second, the effect is gated by circadian 
time, not by time since waking: the paper showed a day-night difference in light-induced adrenal 
gene expression, which is a different claim from a window that closes about an hour after someone 
wakes. 
A 2001 study in humans found that a stepwise transition from under 150 lux to 4,500 lux 
produced a rapid 110-140 nmol/L increase in plasma cortisol in the early morning but not in the 
afternoon, in the absence of any identifiable stressor and without heart rate change. A separate 
report found a roughly 50% cortisol increase with morning exposure between 05:00 and 08:00 and 
no effect with the same intervention between 13:00 and 16:00. The human studies used exposure 
windows of one to three hours and did not test whether responsiveness terminates at a particular 
interval after waking.

Morning Light and Immune Function 
“There’s so much data showing that spiking your morning cortisol this way [through viewing 
sunlight] Establishes low cortisol and high sleepiness hormones, let’s call them, at night, which 
is great.  
 
Buffers stress, and moreover, it kickstart, it kickstarts your immune system to protect you 
against bacteria and viruses you can encounter during the day.” 
Bright morning light can acutely increase cortisol under some experimental conditions, but 
the effect is variable and depends on timing, intensity and duration. A substantial cortisol awakening 
response occurs even in complete darkness, so light modulates an event that happens regardless 
rather than triggering it. Morning light can also phase-advance the circadian rhythm, bringing
melatonin onset earlier on subsequent nights, but there is no evidence that a light-induced morning 
cortisol increase is the mechanism, or that it causes lower nighttime cortisol. 
The circadian cortisol rhythm is thought to contribute to daily immune regulation, including 
immune-cell redistribution. The cortisol awakening response (CAR) specifically has been associated 
with inflammatory markers, though its immune effects have largely been studied indirectly. 
Observational studies link a blunted CAR with higher inflammatory markers, while a recent small 
preprint raises the possibility that overnight immune activation may itself predict a stronger CAR the 
next morning. 
Glucocorticoids can both suppress and enhance aspects of immunity depending on timing, 
dose and context. In mice, physiological glucocorticoid rhythms promote T-cell trafficking, and in cell 
culture cortisol can prime some innate immune responses. Chronic stress and glucocorticoid 
resistance, by contrast, can impair antiviral immunity and prolong inflammation. Not all immune 
markers increase with morning cortisol: salivary IgA falls after awakening as cortisol rises. 
Morning cortisol appears to help organise and regulate immune function, but there is no 
direct evidence that increasing cortisol through morning sunlight "kickstarts" the immune system or 
protects against bacteria and viruses encountered later that day. Establishing this would mean 
tracing a path from light exposure to a rise in cortisol, from that rise to a measurable change in 
immune function, and from that change to fewer infections. That full causal pathway has not been 
demonstrated.

10,000 Lux Lamps and Seasonal Affective Disorder 
“These 10,000 lux lights have been shown to significantly offset the symptoms of seasonal pre- 
uh, affective disorder, et cetera.” 
The standard light therapy regimen for seasonal affective disorder (SAD) involves sitting in 
front of a light box emitting 10,000 lux at approximately 20 cm from the eyes for 30 minutes per day, 
preferably in the morning. Alternative dosing includes 5,000 lux for 1 hour or 2,500 lux for 2 hours.
A meta-analysis of 39 phototherapy studies found that light intensity was positively 
correlated with the antidepressant effect for typical depressive symptoms, but not for atypical 
symptoms such as hypersomnia and carbohydrate craving. This suggests that the lux threshold may 
differ depending on the symptom profile being targeted. 
The discovery that melanopsin-containing photoreceptor cells are maximally sensitive to 
short-wavelength (blue) light challenges the use of photopic lux as a universal dosing metric. When 
blue-enriched spectra are used, far fewer lux are needed to achieve therapeutic effects, because the 
relevant photoreceptors respond to photon flux in the blue range, other than only to photopic 
illuminance. 
A minimum of two weeks of daily light therapy is needed to achieve statistically significant 
symptom reduction, with week 2 showing the largest effect. Standard regimens recommend 
continuing treatment for approximately six weeks at 10,000 lux every morning for 30 minutes. 
Note: Patients with preexisting ocular conditions or those taking photosensitising drugs should 
undergo periodic ophthalmologic examinations during treatment.

The UK Biobank Light Study: Bright Days, Dark Nights and 
Mortality 
“But what we’re finding from this huge study from the UK Biobank, more than 80, I believe it’s 
88 or 86,000 subjects… is that you want bright days and you want dark nights, and they’re 
additive… The risk of dying early if you have bright nights is a, is, goes up immensely, and we 
don’t talk about this enough.” 
A 2024 PNAS paper analysed 88,905 UK Biobank participants (mean age 62.4, 57% female) 
who wore Axivity AX3 wrist-worn light sensors for one week, generating approximately 13 million 
hours of light data. Cause-specific mortality was recorded in 3,750 participants across a mean 
follow-up of 8.0 years. After adjustment for age, sex, ethnicity, photoperiod, and sociodemographic 
and lifestyle factors, higher risk of premature mortality was predicted by brighter nights, darker days, 
suppressed circadian amplitude, and early or late circadian phase.
The night-light association was dose-dependent. Versus the darkest nights, the 70th–90th 
percentile had adjusted hazard ratios of 1.15–1.18, and the brightest 10% had 1.21–1.34 in 
less-adjusted models and 1.21 in the fully adjusted model. In other words, people exposed to the 
most light at night had about a 21% higher risk of the outcome than those exposed to the darkest

UV Index and Eye Damage at Low Sun Angles 
“People who are, are concerned about UV damage should know that when the sun is low in the 
sky, the UV index is much lower, and there’s a reason for that. It’s a phenomenon called 
Rayleigh scattering… When the sun is low in the sky, the UV is, it’s not completely scattered,

The UV index is indeed much lower when the sun is low in the sky, and the risk of sunburn is 
correspondingly lower. However, the claim about eye damage needs nuance and should be separated 
from the sunburn claim.  
The relationship between sun position and eye damage is counterintuitive: the standard UV 
Index (UVI), which measures skin-burn risk, does not reliably predict ocular UV exposure. When the 
sun is low on the horizon, such as in early morning, late afternoon, or throughout winter, direct rays 
enter the eye more easily, making ocular UV exposure higher than the UVI would suggest. At 
maximum solar altitude (midday summer), the brow and eyelids shade the eyes, so most ocular 
exposure comes from scattered and reflected light rather than direct sun. When solar altitude drops 
to roughly 40°, direct rays add to scattered and reflected light, producing peak ocular UV exposure. In
winter, when the sun stays low all day, the ocular UV index (OUVI) remains above the standard UVI 
throughout daylight hours.

Intracellular Melatonin as a Light-Liberated Antioxidant 
“You also have melatonin in most all of the cells of your body, and there it doesn’t make you 
sleepy, it serves as an antioxidant. But it’s sort of captured, and the way to liberate it is through 
long wavelength light. So you have a protective mechanism that’s kicked on with long 
wavelength light.” 
Melatonin is not only a pineal sleep-related hormone; numerous studies describe extrapineal 
melatonin synthesis in tissues throughout the body, and growing evidence suggests that melatonin is 
also produced or concentrated within mitochondria. Unlike circulating pineal melatonin, which has a 
pronounced circadian rhythm regulated by the light–dark cycle, extrapineal melatonin is generally
thought to act locally and is often described as non-circadian or regulated independently of the 
retinal light–dark pathway. 
The antioxidant actions of melatonin are well supported experimentally. Melatonin and 
several of its metabolites can directly scavenge reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, while 
melatonin can also enhance antioxidant defence systems and support mitochondrial redox 
homeostasis. However, the relative importance of these different mechanisms in vivo in humans 
remains less certain. 
There is also emerging evidence that red or near-infrared light may influence melatonin 
production. Animal studies and a small human trial have reported increased melatonin following 
red/NIR exposure, and a 2023 review proposed that NIR could stimulate extrapineal melatonin 
synthesis, potentially in tissues such as the skin. A small study also reported higher salivary 
melatonin following 650-nm red light than 470-nm blue light. However, blue light itself suppresses 
pineal melatonin, so this comparison does not establish that red light actively stimulated melatonin 
synthesis. 
Photobiomodulation is frequently proposed to act through mitochondrial pathways, 
including cytochrome c oxidase, but the exact mechanisms remain debated. Consequently, the 
hypothesis that red/NIR light directly stimulates mitochondrial or extrapineal melatonin production 
in humans remains intriguing and is supported by preliminary evidence.

Indoor LED Lighting and Mitochondrial Damage 
“Indoor LED lights like this, which we all spend a lot of time under, there’s now evidence from, 
from Glenn Jeffrey’s lab and others that it actually may damage the mitochondria.” 
The relevant primary work is a 2021 paper from Jeffery’s group showing that mitochondria 
are specifically vulnerable to 420 nm light in Drosophila, that this undermines their function, and that 
it is associated with reduced fly mobility. Whether ordinary indoor LED lighting damages human 
mitochondria at realistic exposure levels has not been shown. Indoor LED lighting appears most
concerning when it is blue-enriched or lacks longer red/infrared wavelengths, especially in retinal 
and cultured-cell models.  
The strongest evidence is that blue LED light disrupts mitochondrial function through ROS 
and oxidative stress. Reported changes include reduced respiratory-chain activity, lower ATP or 
oxygen consumption, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, and mitochondrial DNA damage. 
Retinal models show the most consistent injury signal. Excessive blue light altered retinal 
mitochondrial structure and function, increased apoptosis, and activated mitochondria-linked death 
pathways in retinal cells and mouse retina.  
Not all LED light has the same effect. Higher-CRI, lower-CCT LEDs with more red content 
preserved more normal mitochondrial function and reduced ROS compared with bluer, high-CCT 
LEDs. Several red-light studies found the opposite of damage, with improved mitochondrial 
respiration or electron-chain activity under specific exposure conditions.

Night Shift Work, Lifespan, Cancer and Metabolic Health 
“Here are the two scariest things. Night shift workers live shorter lives, cancer rates go up, 
they’re metabolically unhealthy, and it’s very hard to mitigate.” 
Night shift work is associated with higher cardiovascular risk and mortality, with recent 
meta-analyses estimating 13% higher total CVD events, 27% higher CVD mortality, and dose-related 
increases with longer exposure. Night shift work is also associated with metabolic syndrome and 
obesity-related abnormalities, including pooled odds ratios around 2.17 in healthcare workers and 
higher triglycerides, blood pressure, BMI, and waist circumference in newer worker cohorts. 
Additionally, cancer risk appears elevated, especially for breast cancer after long-duration or rotating 
night work, but effect sizes are smaller and more heterogeneous than for cardiometabolic outcomes.  
Night shift workers do appear to live shorter lives on average, but the strongest signal is 
cardiovascular mortality, not a huge uniform rise in all-cause mortality across every study. arge 
cohorts found modest all-cause mortality increases overall, with stronger hazards after very long 
exposure, such as 20–30 years of night work in UK Biobank and ≥5 years of rotating night shifts in 
U.S. nurses. 
Cancer risk goes up, but the literature is less clean than the mortality and metabolic 
literature. Reviews consistently point to breast cancer as the main cancer signal, plausibly driven by 
melatonin suppression, circadian disruption, sleep loss, inflammation, and immune changes. 
Long-duration night work shows the clearest pattern, with >10 years and often >20 years of 
exposure. Rotating night work also shows a small but significant increase in breast cancer incidence.

Evening Daylight: Protecting Against Artificial Light at Night 
“When you get some sunlight in your eyes in the late part of your day, let’s just say in the last 
third of your day… You buffer those cells in your eye against the negative effects of artificial 
light at night. This is your Netflix inoculation… you can be on your computer at 9:00 or 10:00 at 
night without it damaging your melatonin levels quite as much.” 
 
“But there’s one population of people for whom this doesn’t work, and it seems to be 
adolescents. You can’t buffer them against the negative effects of screen light at night.” 
Adequate exposure to bright, natural-like light during the day is essential to promote 
circadian entrainment and prepare the body for nighttime rest. Reduced daytime sunlight combined 
with increased evening electrical lighting delays circadian and sleep timing, and insufficient daytime 
light provides only a weak entrainment signal for the internal biological clock.  
Multiple controlled studies confirm that higher prior light exposure reduces subsequent 
sensitivity to evening and nocturnal light. A randomised crossover study in twelve healthy young 
females found that 2.5 hours of early-evening bright light (1200 lx, 18:30–21:00) significantly reduced 
the melatonin-suppressing and alerting effects of a subsequent late-evening light exposure (750 lx, 
22:30–23:30). The same study noted that low daytime light levels over 3–7 days produced larger 
light-induced melatonin suppression at night and that indoor workers with higher 24-hour light 
exposure showed less evening light-induced melatonin suppression than outdoor workers. A 
separate experiment demonstrated that daytime bluish-white light exposure (09:00–10:30) 
prevented significant nocturnal melatonin suppression, whereas daytime dim or white light did not. 
Prior light history also modulates circadian phase shifting. Blue-enriched morning light of 750 
lx produced significantly smaller circadian phase shifts than warm-white morning light of 40 lx, 
indicating that brighter morning exposure attenuates the clock's subsequent responsiveness. A study 
showed three days of very dim light compared to typical indoor illuminance amplified both 
melatonin suppression and phase-resetting responses to a subsequent light stimulus.
Mathematical modelling predicts that robust daytime light exposure confers resilience 
against the circadian-disruptive effects of evening light, reducing both sleep onset latency and 
variability in phase delay attributable to evening screen light. Simulations across a wide range of 
circadian light sensitivities show that brighter daytime light is the most universally beneficial lighting 
intervention, attenuating the phase-delaying effects of evening artificial light. This protection likely 
arises from two mechanisms: photoreceptor pool conversion reducing sensitivity to subsequent light 
and strengthening of circadian amplitude by daytime light exposure. 
Nuances and Limitations 
● Timing matters critically. Afternoon-to-early-evening bright light (4.5 h until 3 h before 
habitual bedtime) paradoxically reduced later melatonin levels and delayed melatonin onset 
by ~21 min in adolescents, suggesting that the bright light itself acutely phase-delayed the 
circadian system. 
● Prior-day light history differs from same-day exposure. Bright light recorded over the ~32 h 
before laboratory entry was associated with higher evening melatonin and earlier onset, 
likely because it included morning light with phase-advancing properties. 
● Photosensitivity adaptation may require days, not hours. In adolescents, circadian 
photosensitivity adaptation for melatonin suppression may not manifest within hours, as 
some adult studies suggest, but rather over a couple of days. 
● Seasonal variation modulates the effect. Melatonin suppression by moderate light was 
stronger in winter than in summer, which is attributed to less daylight exposure and a lower 
prior light history in winter. 
The cumulative evidence indicates that brighter daytime and early-evening light can 
attenuate subsequent melatonin suppression and circadian phase shifts, but the protective effect 
depends on the timing, duration, spectral composition, and temporal proximity of the prior light 
exposure to the subsequent light stimulus.
