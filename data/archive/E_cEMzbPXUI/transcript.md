It walks, it lifts, it grips a cup without crushing it. Most people watch those clips and assume the race is about intelligence, about who builds the smartest brain. The brain is hard, no question. Now everybody is missing the body and the body depends on one small part that comes almost entirely from China. In 2025, Beijing proved it could choke with that supply. Within weeks it slowed Tesla's robot line. Strip away the science fiction shell and a humanoid robot is a stack of small electric motors. Tesla's robot Optimus carries between 40 and 50 of them. There is roughly one in every joint. Each hand alone bends 22 different ways, close to a human hand. Every motor has to be small enough to fit a human frame. It also has to be strong enough to move a human load. Those two demands fight each other. The thing that settles that fight is the magnet. The magnets are made mostly from neodymium with iron and boron. Add a pinch of heavy metals and that keeps them working when they heat up. These are rare earth magnets. They are the strongest magnets we know how to make, way stronger than the ones you have on your fridge. That strength is what lets a compact motor produce real torque. Drop in a weaker magnet and the motor grows bigger, it runs hotter, or gives up power. None of those work inside an arm meant to lift and hold all day. Strength in a small package is the whole game, and only one family of magnets delivers it. A single Optimus uses about 3.5 kg of these magnets across its joints. Most humanoid designs have between two and four kilograms. Optimus sits near the top of that range. It sounds small until you multiply it out. Tesla talks about building millions of robots, and its rivals talk in the same range. At that scale, robot motors alone would swallow more high-performance magnet supply than whole industries use today. Robots fill this more than almost any machine before them. A car needs one or two big motors. A phone needs a handful of tiny ones. A humanoid needs dozens, each demanding high force in a tight space. That is the exact spot where a cheaper magnet fails. So, the smallest, most critical part of the robot is also the most concentrated in supply. Who makes it, and what happens if they stop? China did not stumble into this. Starting in 1990s, they built every step of the magnet supply chain. Mining, chemistry, the finished parts, all of it grew at home whilst Western producers shut down or never started. By the time magnets turned critical for electric cars, wind turbines, and robots, the supply already sat in one country. Japan holds a slim share of the heavy magnet market, near 10%, and even that leans on raw material from China. For years, this went unnoticed. In April 2025, it stopped going unnoticed. The move didn't come from nowhere. Weeks earlier, Washington had raised steep tariffs on Chinese goods, and Beijing answered with one lever the West could not match. It put seven rare earth elements under an export license along with the finished magnets themselves. The list included the heavy metals like heavy metals that let a magnet survive the heat inside a working motor. On paper, it was a safety measure with the materials labeled dual use, meaning fit for weapons as well as washing machines. In practice, it was a switch. Anything carrying those magnets now needed Beijing's approval to leave the country and the approval came slowly on a timeline of weeks or months. The effect landed fast. On Tesla's earnings call that month, Elon Musk told investors that Optimus production had been hit by what he called a magnet issue. China wanted assurance the magnets would not go towards weapons, he said, and Tesla was working through the license. Musk summed up the whole problem in one line, "A robot moves only as fast as its slowest and least lucky part." The cleverest software on earth means nothing if the joints are stuck in a warehouse waiting on a permit. Tesla was not alone in feeling it. Ford paused a plant in Chicago when its own magnet supply ran short. Other manufacturers reported the same squeeze. Buyers who had treated these magnets as an ordinary commodity learned that they were a single point of failure. For a few moments in 2025, a decision in one capital in Beijing set the pace of factories on the other side of the world. This was not the first time Beijing reached for the valve. Back in 2020 10, during a dispute with Japan over a chain of islands, China choked off rare earth shipments to its neighbor. Prices spiked, by some measures close to sevenfold, before the controls eased. The world treated it as a one-off. Really, it was a rehearsal. After that scare, Japan and a few others swore they would diversify. Some projects started, most stalled within a few years. By 2015, China had relaxed its grip. Prices fell back and the urgency drained away. The West drifted back to the cheapest supplier and the dependence only deepened. The lesson of 2010 sat there to be learned. Almost nobody learned from it. The standoff in 2025 later cooled and the licenses began to flow again. The moment they did, American magnet imports from China jumped more than 600% in a single month. Buyers were scrambling to rebuild their stockpiles. That rebound told its own story because the dependence had not gone away. The point was never that China would cut the West off forever. The point is that it can at a time of its choosing on terms it sets. So, the obvious answer is the one every Western planner reached for next. If the dependence is this dangerous, why not build the supply at home? The instinct is to dig more mines. That instinct misreads the problem. Rare earth elements are not actually rare. They sit in ordinary rock all over the planet including across the United States. The trouble is not pulling them out of the ground. It is pulling them apart from each other because the 17 rare earth elements are chemically almost identical and bond together in the same ore. Separating them into pure magnet grade material takes a long run of chemical steps. Few places can do it cleanly, cheaply and scale. The process is also dirty and that is part of why the West let it go. The chemistry throws off acids and low level radioactive waste and the cleanup is expensive. Decades ago on cost and pollution, America and European producers walked away. China did not. Much of its supply now runs through one giant complex in Inner Mongolia called Bayan Obo, I think I pronounced that right, the largest of rare earth deposit on earth. The result is a lopsided map. China controls roughly 70% of the world's rare earth mining. It controls close to 90% of the refining and magnet making that comes after. The mining is actually the easy end. The refining and the finished magnet are where the real control sits and the gap is widest for the heavy metals. Almost all of the heavy metals that keep a magnet alive in a hot motor are separated inside China. Those are the very metals it placed under license. Rebuilding this at home is slow by nature. A new western mine can take years just to win its permits. From first discovery to real production, industry average runs close to, wait for it, 17 years. A refining and magnet plant then adds more time on top. Money is not the missing piece because money has already arrived. Venture funding for robotics roughly tripled between 2023 and 2025 reaching about $40 billion a year. Almost none of it can buy a faster refining chain. What it cannot buy is skill and China holds most of that, too. China has filed the bulk of the world's patents in this field. On humanoid parts, it leads the United States by a wide margin. By one count, near 7,700 filings over 5 years against roughly 1,560. Beijing has also aimed a state fund worth about $138 billion at artificial intelligence and robots. The lead is being widened on purpose. None of this leaves the west stuck. It means the fix has to be deliberate, funded, and where the patent. Several western producers tried to rebuild the chain over the past 20 years and stalled partway. Consider what restarting really means. You need chemists who have run the separation at scale. You need engineers who can keep the waste in check. And you need buyers willing to pay above the Chinese price for years while a new plant finds its feet. Lose that chain just once and it does not snap back easily. The skill walks out the door with the people who had it. Then, in 2025, the United States finally started acting like it understood the size of the job. The clearest sign came from an unlikely spot. A single mine in the California desert and a phone call from the Pentagon. The mine is Mountain Pass in California, and the company that runs it is MP Materials. For years, it was the only large rare earth mine working in the United States. It nearly died last decade when its previous owner went bankrupt in 2015. Even after it came back, it often shipped its ore to China for the hard refining step. That is how deep the reliance ran. America could dig the rock and still had to send it abroad to make full use of it. In July 2025, that changed. The US Department of Defense did more than write a grant. It bought $400 million of preferred stock, took a stake near 15%, and became the company's largest shareholder. Weeks later, it added a $150 million loan. It set a floor price near $110 a kilogram for key materials so a future wave of cheap Chinese supply could not bankrupt the business. And it agreed to buy a decade of output from a planned new magnet plant called Tenex. The site is now set for North Lake, Texas. The plant aims to make about 7,000 tons of magnets a year once it opens in 2028, lifting the company's total to around 10,000 tons. The wider goal is blunt. The Pentagon wants Chinese rare earths out of American defense supply chains by 2027. Days later, Apple added its own move. It signed a $500 deal with MP Materials and prepaid $200 of it. The agreement lines up American-made magnets for hundreds of millions of Apple devices from 2027. It also funds a recycling line at Mountain Pass, pulling rare earths back out of old hardware. MP has already making finished rare earth magnets at its Texas plant. It's begun drawing feedstock from Saudi Arabia and other partners, so the supply does not lean on one source. The company is not yet profitable and its revenue grew only about 10% in 2025. Even so, its share price climbed sharply as the deals landed. The market had decided that owning this chain was worth a premium. MP is not the only one building a second venture. US Rare Earth is putting up its own magnet plants. Lynas, an Australian firm and the largest rare earth processor outside China, is building a plant in Texas. Recyclers like Novion, which raised $215 million, are working to reclaim magnets from scrap rather than mine them fresh. Mining companies rarely see governments buy a stake in their operations. When a state takes that step, it views the underlying material as a vital matter of national security. And the reason runs wider than robotics. The same magnets sit inside drones, fighter jets, missiles, and the motors of nuclear submarines. A single F-35 fighter holds more than 900 lb of rare earth material, and Arleigh a destroyer needs roughly 5,200. One Virgin Class submarine holds around 9,200 lb. Stockpiling raw orders not fix this because a contractor cannot bolt unrefined rock into a fighter jet. What the Pentagon needs is finished magnets, and for now those still come from China. China makes nearly all of the world's samarium, the heat-resistant magnet metal that goes into missiles and electronic warfare gear. The same magnets also sit inside satellites and the hardware SpaceX launches by the thousand. Elon Musk runs companies on both sides of that civilian and military line. That is part of why China asked for assurances in the first place. Seen that way, the magnet stopped being a parts problem. It became a question of national security. The Pentagon was not protecting one robot, it was protecting the freedom to build robots, jets, and rockets without asking another government for a permit. Right, back to it because some engineers reckon they can design this magnet problem out of the robot completely, and that's where it gets good. Even so, a mine and plant take years to reach full output, and the robot timelines will not wait. That leaves one faster route worth testing. Could engineers design the magnet out of the robot altogether? Tesla saw this coming earlier than most. In 2023, the company said its next motor would use no rare earth elements at all. That plan leans on ferrites, a cheap and common magnet material with clever design making up the lost strength. It was a bold promise, and it points the right way. Three years on, the promise is still mostly a promise. As of 2026, no rare earth free Tesla drive unit has reached production, and that reason is physics, not through lack of trying. Ferrites magnets are far weaker than rare earth magnets. To match the same power, you need a bigger, heavier motor, and weight is the one thing a walking robot cannot spare. A car can carry a heavier motor without much cost. A humanoid joint is the opposite. It asks for the most force in the least space, which is the hardest place in the whole machine to give up magnet strength. A more promising path runs through a Minnesota company called Niron Magnets. Niron builds permanent magnets from iron and nitrogen, two cheap and abundant materials with no rare earths inside. It's the first company in the world to bring this kind of magnet to real production. Its first plan is scaling towards 1,500 tons a year. It has announced a second site backed by about 1.8 billion dollars aimed at up to 10,000 tons per year. The car maker Stellantis has been invested since 2023, and the Department of Energy added an additional 17.5 million dollar award. The CEO even went before a House Committee on China to pitch iron nitride as America's way off the dependence. The company opened a Washington office to stay close to the agencies writing the checks. Now, an honest take on this is that it helps, although not everywhere at once. Iron nitride magnets are strong enough today for speakers, sensors, and many ordinary motors. That is where they will arrive first. Niron's earliest products are loudspeaker magnets, not robot joints, and its near-term output would only cover 1 to 2% of the global magnet market. The hardest job is a high torque robot joint. That is the last one these magnets will reach. It needs the most performance in the smallest package. There is a third route that needs no new mine at all. The magnets which currently exist in the world can be used twice. Old hard drives, wind turbines, and scrapped electric motors are full of neodymium. A Canadian-American firm called Materials is building plants in Arizona and Ontario to pull it back out. Today, less than 1% of the world's rare earths gets recycled. By 2035, the United States alone could hold more than 43,000 tons of old magnets worth reclaiming. That will not cover robot boom by itself, but every ton recovered at home is a ton that does not wait on a permit from Beijing.
