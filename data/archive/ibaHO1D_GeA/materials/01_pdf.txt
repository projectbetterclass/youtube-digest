Change	is	coming
¨ The	US	tax	code	is	broken	and	needed	change:	The	US	corporate	
tax	code,	as	it	existed	in	2017,	was	an	abomination,	a	carry	over	
from	a	prior	century	where	the	US	was	the	center	of	the	global	
economy	and	companies	would	do	anything	demanded	of	them,	to	
preserve	their	US	incorporation.	
¨ This	change	is	imperfect:	While	the	process	was	messy	and	
partisan,	it	represents	the	most	significant	change	in	corporate	
taxation	in	the	United	States	in	the	my	lifetime,	and	as	with	all	tax	
reform,	it	is	a	mix	of	the	good,	the	bad	and	the	ugly,	with	your	
political	priors	determining	which	one	you	believe	dominates.	
¨ But	it	was	have	consequences:	No	matter	what	you	think	about	the	
tax	reform	package,	there	is	the	one	thing	that	is	not	debatable:	it	
will	impact	equity	value	and	affect	corporate	behavior	in	the	
coming	year.
3
Tax	Code	Change	1:	Corporate	Tax	Rate
¨ The	federal	corporate	tax	rate	on	the	income	that	
corporations	generate	ion	the	United	States	has	
been	lowered	from	35%,	at	the	federal	level,	to	21%.	
¨ This	is	the	portion	of	the	bill	that	attracted	the	most	
media	attention,	primarily	because	of	the	magnitude	
of	the	drop,	bringing	corporate	taxes	in	the	United	
States	down	to	levels	not	seen	in	the	country	since	
the	second	world	war.
¨ These	corporate	tax	rates	move	the	US	closer	to	the	
rest	of	the	world.	The	median	corporate	tax	rate	
globally	is	about	25%.
4
Tax	Code	Change	2:	Treatment	of	foreign	
income
¨ Shift	from	global	tax	to	regional	tax	model:	Until	2017,	US	
companies	were	required	to	pay	the	US	tax	rate	on	all	of	their	
global	income,	though	the	differential	tax	on	foreign	income	
does	not	have	to	be	paid,	until	repatriated	to	the	US.	Starting	
in	2018,	US	companies	will	have	to	pay	only	the	foreign	taxes	
due	on	foreign	income	and	will	be	free	to	bring	the	money	
back,	when	they	want.
¨ Un-trap	cash:	One	time	tax	on	trapped	cash,	from	foreign	
income	not	repatriated	back	in	earlier	years:	15.5%	on	liquid	
assets	and	8%	on	illiquid	assets.
¨ Restrictions	on	intangible	assets:	Companies	will	be	a	
minimum	tax	rate	of	13.1%	(rising	to	16.4%	in	2025)	on	
excess	profits	(over	and	above	a	10%	cost	of	capital)	earned	
in	foreign	subsidiaries.
5
Tax	Code	Change	3:	Restrictions	on	
Interest	Tax	Deduction	
¨ Interest	expense	deduction	capped:	For	the	first	
time,	the	US	tax	code	will	put	a	limit	on	the	
deductibility	of	interest	expenses,	restricting	it	to	
30%	of	the	"adjusted	taxable	income"	(with	taxable	
income	defined	as	EBITDA	through	2022	and	EBIT	
thereafter).	
¨ Can	be	carried	forward:	To	provide	a	cushion	for	
companies	that	may	have	cyclical	income,	the	lost	
(non-tax	deductible)	interest	expense	deductions	
can	be	carried	forward	and	used	in	future	years,	with	
no	expiration	date.
6
Tax	Code	Change	4:	Capital	Expensing	
¨ Capital	Expenditures	will	be	tax	deductible	in	full:	US	
companies	will	be	allowed	to	deduct	their	
investments	in	tangible	assets	in	the	year	of	the	
investment,	for	taxable	income	calculations,	rather	
than	have	to	depreciate	it	over	time.	
¨ Provision	will	be	phased	out:	This	provision	will	
remain	intact	until	2023	and	be	phased	out	by	2027.

A	Pricing	Caution
¨ Priced	in	already?	While	the	final	package	contained	some	
surprises,	the final	version	of	the	bill	preserved	the	key	ingredients	
that	we	introduced	in	April	2017.	The	strong	returns	posted	by	US	
stocks	last	year	already	include	some	of	the	value	effects	of	the	tax	
law.	
¨ Business	changes	coming:	Note	that	this	does	not	mean	that	the	
effects	of	the	new	tax	code	have	already	worked	their	way	into	
prices	since	we	still	do	not	know	how	companies	or	the	US	
economy	will	respond	to	the	changes.	
¨ Dynamic? This	analysis	is	static,	insofar	as	it	does	not	allow	for	the	
changes	in	investing,	financing	and	dividend	behavior	that	we	will	
see,	as	a	consequence	of	the	tax	change.	For	instance,	firms	may	
decrease	how	much	they	borrow,	since	the	tax	benefit	to	debt	has	
decreased,	and	that	will	lower	debt	ratios	and	change	the	cost	of	
capital	further.

The	Trifectas
¨ The	Benefit	Trifecta:	Companies	(sectors)	that	are	
currently	paying	high	effective	tax	rates,	invest	large	
amounts	in	tangible	(depreciable)	assets	and	have	
little	or	no	debt	will	benefit	the	most	from	the	tax	
code	changes
¨ The	Pain	Trifecta:	Companies	 (sectors)	that	are	
currently	paying	low	effective	tax	rates,	invest	little	
or	nothing	in	tangible	(depreciable)	assets	and	have	
high	debt	will	be	hurt	the	most	by	the	tax	code	
changes.

The	Unanswered	Questions	
¨ Taxable	income	and	tax	rates:	 Facing	a	more	benign	domestic	tax	
environment,	will	companies	to	be	more	expansive	in	their	
measurement	of	taxable	income?	 How	much	of	this	income	will	
they	pay	out	in	effective	taxes?
¨ Capital	Expenditures	in	tangible	asset	sectors:	The	capital	
expensing	provision	should	make	it	more	investing	in	depreciable	
assets	more	attractive,	but	will	that	be	sufficient	to	induce	
companies	to	reinvest	more?	If	so,	how	much?
¨ The	Untrapping of	Cash:	How	much	of	the	trapped	cash	will	
companies	bring	back	home,	paying	the	one-time	tax	penalty?	Will	
they	reinvest	this	cash	or	return	it	(in	the	form	of	dividends	and	
buybacks)?
¨ The	Debt	Shift:	Will	highly	levered	businesses	react	to	the	
reduction	in	tax	benefits	from	debt	by	retiring	debt?	What	effects	
will	a	system-wide	delevering have	on	bond	default	spreads?
