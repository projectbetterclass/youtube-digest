# Should you use BFR cuffs?

If you don't know what this is, I'll let me explain it real quick and whether or not you should be using it in your leg training. BFR training stands for blood flow restricted training. And it's exactly what it sounds like. It's basically where you use a designed BFR tool, which you can get for cheap on Amazon, or you can get creative with a belt or something like that, or just a rope, and you tie it around the higher part of a segment. So, in this example right here, Hailey has it below her butt cheeks and above her quads. So, because the BFR is wrapped up here, it will basically restrict blood flow into and out of the muscle. So, since she has the cuff here, it limits blood flow to the quads. Because blood flow is limited into and out of the quads, if she does something like a leg extension or just a body weight squat, the limited blood flow supply basically makes it more difficult for the muscle to create tons of force. And what ends up being the net benefit of this can challenge the action of the muscle, knee extension in the case of the quads, with a lot less load and get the same potential results. This has been shown across multiple studies, but as far as we currently understand, blood flow restricted training can be just as effective as traditional resistance training, at least for muscle growth. So, this can be really useful in three different cases. Number one, if you're traveling and are limited on equipment and you really only have a couple of options, say for lower body, you can do a BFR cuff strategy, so we can only really use your body weight or something like that for your leg work. And blood flow to your quads and hamstrings and even calves will be limited and you don't have to use as much load. You can maybe even do body weight squats or body weight leg curls or something like that and get a great stimulus basically no equipment. The second thing is if you have some kind of joint sensitivity, like let's say someone has a knee issue and you want to decrease the amount of load that you can use on an exercise, cuffing using a BFR cuff is a great way to do that. You could apply the same thing to biceps and triceps training, where you would cuff above the biceps and triceps, so that blood flow would be restricted to either muscle and you wouldn't have to use as much weight to get the same outcome. And then number three is just for fun. Like if you're someone who loves a really good pump and a really good burn and wants to feel a muscle really, really significantly and get a huge, huge sensation in the muscle, this is another great way to do that. It's just fun. The potential downsides of BFR training are twofold. Number one, it's inconvenient and super [ __ ] uncomfortable. And you also just can't do it for every muscle. Like for instance, if you wanted to try to do it with your glutes, there would be no practical way to really do that. So it only works for some muscles and it's super uncomfortable, which can be a downside if you're not that kind of person. And then number two, the strength-related benefits of normal weight training are going to be limited with BFR training. But again, that can be an upside if you're someone who's dealing with injury or maybe recovering from a surgery or something similar. My personal recommendation is don't worry too much about the BFR stuff and applying it. It's not like it's going to provide any sort of massive, awesome, amazing additional benefit to your normal training. But in these particular cases, travel and injury especially, it can be incredibly useful as a tool in your tool belt. Shout out Haley Beebs.
