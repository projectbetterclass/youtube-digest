One reason companies expect more use is that the price of using AI has been falling. A token is a small chunk of text an AI model processes and customers can be charged according to how many tokens they use. Smaller and mid-tier models have typically seen bigger price cuts while flagship list prices have been slower to change. Your first thought might be that cheaper tokens means less money coming in and fewer machines needed, but a lower cost can make a job worth doing when it didn't make sense before. A business can justify an expense answer for work like complex engineering if the value of that answer covers the cost. For routine paperwork on a low margin product, the same price might make no sense. Now drop that price by let's say 10 times and more of those everyday jobs become worth automating. A small business could use AI to deal with routine customer inquiries rather than saving it for its most expensive problems. Or an agent could keep working through paperwork in the background throughout the day. Each job might cost less, but there can be many more jobs being done and every new job still needs computing power, which is what the data center provides. So the companies building them are betting that the increase in use will justify the extra capacity. Back in 1865, the English economist William Stanley Jevons described a similar effect with coal. Steam engines became more efficient, yet Britain burned more coal rather than less. Cheaper power made machinery worth installing in places where it hadn't made sense before. So using less fuel for each job could encourage enough additional work to increase total fuel use. That's the historical argument behind the bet.
