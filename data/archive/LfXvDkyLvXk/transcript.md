The most important policy moment in this story happened in October 2022. The Biden administration issued what became known as the October 7th chip ban, the most sweeping export controls on semiconductor technology in decades. The rules banned the sale of advanced chip manufacturing equipment to China. They barred US companies and their foreign subsidiaries from supporting Chinese advanced chip production without a license. They cut off China's access to the highest performance AI chips, specifically Nvidia's A100 and H100 chips, and the tools needed to make equivalent chips domestically. The intent was clear, keep China at least two generations behind in AI hardware, buying time for the US to consolidate its lead. That didn't work very well. And why was that? Not because the controls were poorly designed, they were actually quite sophisticated, but because of what happened 11 months later. In late August 2023, Huawei, which had been cut off from advanced chips, quietly put a new smartphone on the market, the Mate 60 Pro. There was no press conference, no marketing fanfare. It just quietly appeared on shelves. A few weeks later, the research firm TechInsights bought one and tore it apart. What they found inside was a chip called Kirin 9000S, built at 7 nanometers by China's domestic chip manufacturer SMIC. 7 nanometers without EUV machines, using an older class of lithography called DUV, deep ultraviolet. And that machine was pushed far beyond what most engineers thought was possible through a process of repeated passes called multi-patterning that essentially approximates more advanced lithography by running the same step multiple times. The yields were not great, roughly 50% compared to TSMC's early 7 nanometer yields of around 76%. The wafer costs were roughly double TSMC's equivalent, but the chips worked. It powered a flagship smartphone. It had a functioning 5G modem, and it shocked Washington so badly that multiple congressional hearings were followed. The message was unmistakable. Now, there is real truth in the efficiency thesis, but the Jevons paradox complicates it. Named after the 19th century British economist William Stanley Jevons, who observed that as coal-burning steam engines became more fuel-efficient, total coal consumption went up, not down, because efficiency made the coal useful for more applications than before. The same dynamic applies here. Cheaper inference doesn't reduce hardware demand, it expands what people try to do with AI, which pulls demand back up. Every company that couldn't afford to deploy AI at scale last year can now afford it this year. Every new use case that opens up requires more compute.
