§I am a dabbler, by nature, moving on from topic to topic, but this 
year, I have spent more time than usual on one topic, AI, but it 
seems to be part of almost every business or economy related 
story. 
§I started my analysis of how AI would affect the rest of us, from 
doomsday to do-nothing scenarios, largely in response to Citrini and 
Citadel reports on the topic. in August 2026, I took my first big picture 
look at AI as a business, which I titled its bar mitzvah moment, with a 
look at its potential market, business economics and moats. 
§Just last week, I argued that both Anthropic and OpenAI would be 
priced, rather than valued, for their initial public offerings, using what I 
called buzzy metrics (like annualized run rates) and promised that I 
would try to value the company, when it filed its prospectus. 
§On October 26, 2026, Anthropic filed its prospectus, and at the 
risk of being called hopelessly wrong by both bulls and bears, I 
will try to value the company, using what I see as its most likely 
pathway, while also leaving the door open for you to disagree with 
me, and pick your own pathways to valuing the company.

§Revenue growth
§Pluses: The company is growing quickly, and for at least of the subset of users that have tried its products, it 
is delivering on its promise. 
§Minuses: The revenue growth rate is clearly coming down, as scale, competition and compute power 
constraints all work against the company, and the fact that it is so interconnected in both operating and 
financing with other big players in the AI game does lead to the potential for double counting and 
opacity.
§Profitability
§Pluses: The expenses for Anthropic, across categories, are growing slower than revenues and that is 
helping reduce operating losses and putting it on a glide path to not just profitability, but significant 
profitability. 
§Minuses: Notwithstanding one quarter's numbers, the company is not quite ready to deliver sustained 
profits yet and  will require significant revenue growth to keep ahead of the expenses that will hit the 
income statement from compute power commitments.
§Reinvestment for Growth
§Pluses: The immense compute power commitments taken on by Anthropic is largely responsible for the 
high gross profit margins reported by the company, because they have moved company's biggest expense 
from the variable to the fixed operating cost column. 
§Minuses: Not only does this increase the company's operating leverage, but also will result in further 
commitments down the road, as compute power needs to be replenished and grown.
§Risk
§Pluses: The company has built a buffer against risk in the form of a very large cash balance, which will be 
added to in the IPO, and it is is possible that companies will become reliant on its products, 
§Minuses: It is exposed to risk on three dimensions - in operations, from high operating leverage, in financing, 
because of its contractual content cost commitments and on survival, since it is dependent on growth to 
meet its obligations.

§I believe that AI will have the power to disrupt some 
industries, and that its most powerful (and highest priced) 
models will displace white collar (and highly paid) 
employees, yielding an end market size of $5 trillion or greater 
for its products.
§ In my view, and that is based on a very early reading of the AI 
business today, Anthropic is aiming for the premium portion of 
the market and looks like a leader in that portion of the 
market. 
§The one emerging add on is Anthropic's ambition (made explicit 
in the prospectus) to be a player in a subset of the businesses that 
its agents are disruption, with the pharmaceutical business 
emerging as a potential play. 
§With that story in place, I believe that it is plausible that 
Anthropic's revenues could approach a trillion dollars in a 
decade. For perspective, there is only no company in the 
world with more than a trillion in revenues today , with 
Amazon and Walmart sitting at around $700-$750 billion.

§To estimate where margins will converge, it may help to start 
with the recognition that the AI product and service market, 
once established, is likely to have 
§a mass market segment where models, many of which may be 
open-sourced and less powerful, will compete for customers 
primarily on pricing, and 
§a premium segment, where frontier models, many of which will 
remain closed models, powered by client data, dominate. 
§The operating margins for the former are more likely to 
resemble those of business services (roughly 10%), whereas 
the operating margins for the latter will converge on the 
operating margins of enterprise software (close to 40%), with 
compute power and R&D becoming the reinvestment needed to 
deliver these products.
15
§Anthropic is targeting the premium market, as reflected not 
only in its current revenue breakdown, with almost 80% from 
enterprises paying based on usage, but also in its focus on 
using its newly acquired compute power to build next 
generation models rather than service current clients. 
§I will assume an end-mix of 75% of revenues from 
premium products, and 25% from the mass market, giving 
me an unadjusted pre-tax operating margin of 32.5%; that 
would put them at the ninth decile of US companies in 
2026.

§The nature of compute power, and especially with the chips 
that represent a large percentage of costs, is that investments 
will age quickly and will need replacement, which is an 
indication that if Anthropic's growth ambitions come to fruition, 
there will be more reinvestment needed in future years, to both 
replenish and grow compute power. 
§It is true that technology may make chips cheaper, but that 
may be more than offset by new models demanding 
proportionately more power, and if you add in the real estate 
and power needs, there is room for disagreement. 
§I do believe that the compute power crunch will stay in place for 
the near term, but will ease over time, and that belief translates 
into my assumption that Anthropic will need to invest about $1 
for every $3 in additional revenues, going forward. That would 
be in line with what technology companies are generating as 
revenues per dollar of invested capital today , but with the leap 
in revenues that Anthropic is forecasting, it will translate into 
large dollar reinvestment each year.

§The AI business is still evolving, and while Anthropic's growth and 
customer stickiness are noteworthy, I am just being realistic when I 
contend that the business will face challenges as it grows, and 
lives through market and economic turmoil. 
§Thus, for companies spending on AI as a tool, we do not know 
whether they will cut back their spending, and if so how much, 
in bad times, and for those who plan to replace employees with AI 
agents, whether the pace and extent of usage will change with the 
economy. 
§The number that is designed to convey this risk into the valuation 
is the cost of capital, and for Anthropic, assuming that it carries 
the risk of large technology companies with an added burden 
of the compute commitments, I will estimate a cost of capital 
of 9.74%. Again, for perspective, note that the median cost of 
capital for US firms in September 2026 was 9.16% and that 
9.74% is close to the third quartile (of 9.88%).

§The main talking point for many Anthropic bears is the compute 
commitment that runs into hundreds of billions, creating fixed 
costs that will be a drag on profitability, especially if revenue 
growth falls short of expectations, as well as the risk of distress. 
§While bondholders are right to be trepidatious about this 
possibility and will point to the current bond rating of BB+ for the 
company as backing, there are two reasons why failure risk will be 
suppressed. 
§The first is that the company has the cash balance to sustain itself 
through the near terms and meet its contractual obligations. 
§The second is that, as a high-profile trillion-dollar plus company, it will 
have access to capital. 
§I do believe, though, that if revenues grow at anemic rates, both 
these benefits will dissipate, as cash gets burned through and the 
market cap collapses, and I will estimate a probability of failure 
of 10% for the company , with a loss of half its value if that 
happen.

§With AI companies, the question with regulatory risk is not 
whether it will show up, but when it does, what form it will 
take. 
§Anthropic and OpenAI have both invited the government to 
restrict how this business will grow, and while there may be 
the cynical rationale of keeping out competition (especially 
from China) for these pleas, the regulations and restrictions are 
coming. 
§As with social media companies, the effect of these regulations 
will not be a cessation of the business but an increase in 
operating expenses and perhaps some lost revenues. 
§I estimate that the regulatory effects are likely to be mild, 
at least on a relative basis, and that it will shave my initial 
operating margin estimate of 32.5%, for Anthropic, down to 
27.5% (a reduction of 5%).

§The worst-case Anthropic story , where target revenues limp to 
$200 billion in 2036, margins converge on the mass market (10%), 
compute power stays scarce and costly (sales to capital of one), 
cost of capital rises to the third quartile for US companies (9.88%) 
and the regulatory effect on margins is drastic yields worthless 
equity, In fact, the value of equity is -$373 billion, and the 
company will cease to exist.
§The best-case Anthropic story, where target revenues surge to 
$1.5 trillion in 2036, margins converge on the premium market 
(40%), compute power becomes cheap (sales to capital of four), 
cost of capital falls to the first quartile for US companies (7.79%) 
and the regulatory effect on margins is non-existent yields a value 
of $4.04 trillion for equity .
§While I believe that the best case and worse case scenarios are 
both unlikely, you can assess your own story for the company, 
with the resulting value, applying what I call the 3P test: Is it 
possible? Is it plausible? Is it probable? Investing is a personal 
decision, and whether you choose to buy Anthropic, sell it short or 
sit on the sidelines, you should base it on your own assessment.

§he company has structured itself as a public benefit 
corporation, and in its prospectus, it states that "we may make 
decisions that we believe appropriately balance the pecuniary 
interests of our stockholders, the best interests of those materially 
affected by our conduct, and the Company Purpose, but that 
negatively impact the short-, medium-, or long-term performance of 
our business or the market price". 
§Given the worries that people have about AI being a force for 
destruction, a concern that Dario Amodei has actively cultivated, 
this protection against shareholder power may seem to be a good 
thing, but we need to be open eyed about what this structure does. 
§It does not protect society from AI's evils, but puts that power 
in the hands of the few (the founders and members of the benefit 
trust) at the top of Anthropic, hoping that they use that power well. 
If you, as an investor, have had bad experiences with top 
management at companies using corporate responsibility and ESG 
as cover for self-serving activity and hypocrisy, the Anthropic 
structure is that structure on steroids.

§At the IPO, Anthropic will be priced, and that almost everyone who trades the 
stock right after the offering will be pricing, not valuing the stock. 
§If it is priced well above my estimate of $1.48 trillion, I will sit out the frenzy. 
To the question of why I would not sell short, if it is priced well above my value, 
my response is that while I have faith in my valuation, with a range around my 
estimate, I have no reason to believe that the market will see the error of its 
ways, if it has made an error, and correct within my time horizon.
§Once Anthropic starts trading, the story will continue, and given AI's 
presence in the economy and markets, I can guarantee you that it will be a 
roller coaster. It is possible that we have underestimated the possible payoff 
from AI, and that Anthropic continues to rise in market cap, but it is just as 
possible that major parts of the storyline, from revenue growth to margins 
to risk, will come under stress, leading the market to reassess what it 
thinks Anthropic should be priced at. 
§Obviously, those stresses will also cause my estimate of the value to change, but 
it not just a possibility but almost a guarantee that there will be a day that 
Anthropic will be undervalued, and it may be sooner than you think. The 
structure of the company, with the public benefit facade and the voting rights 
abuse, will require a much larger discount on my value estimate, but the market 
may accommodate that requirement as well.
