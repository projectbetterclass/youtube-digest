## [English was my THIRD language 🤯](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/l0CE9VexfVA)

**Vinh Giang** · 🟡 Skim
> Practical and honest advice, but the core points (immersion + repetition = fluency) are widely known and don't require 60 seconds to capture.

**Key points**
- English fluency came from making it his primary language—speaking it 80%+ of the time, not just in formal settings
- Built immersion by intentionally making English-speaking friends and avoiding speaking Vietnamese/Chinese during breaks at school
- Used self-talk (30-minute solo speaking sessions) as a practical workaround when fewer English-speaking friends were available
- Core principle: 'You get good at what you practice'—language skill correlates directly with usage volume
- Addressed the math: speaking English 2–3 hours per week while using native language 50+ hours makes progress hard

**Takeaway:** Language fluency requires making your target language your primary daily language through deliberate social and solo practice; volume and repetition matter far more than study method.

**🔍 Reality check:** Vinh's advice reflects his own path (multilingual home, access to English-speaking peers, time/motivation to practice solo), which may not generalize equally to learners with different social circumstances, age, or time constraints. Self-talk and peer immersion are both real techniques but require discipline and opportunity—the video frames them as simple but doesn't acknowledge that access to English speakers or blocks of free time are privileges not everyone has. No major overclaim, but presented more as universal formula than as one learner's successful strategy.

## [Interest rates and Stock Prices:  An Old Market Debate Replayed!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sPUonzHWZEY)

**Aswath Damodaran** · ✅ Watch in full
> Rigorous, data-driven analysis using 70+ years of historical context, real-time 2026 breakdowns, and cross-country comparison; Damodaran carefully deconstructs a widely misunderstood relationship and provides actionable framing for investors.

**Key points**
- US Treasury rates have risen to 20-year highs in 2026 (around 4.75%), but remain lower than longer historical averages (1954–present), suggesting current levels are closer to 'normal' than the 2008–2021 period of near-zero rates.
- Higher rates reflect primarily sticky inflation expectations (anchored around 2.5–3%), not Fed action; global government bond rates have risen in tandem across major developed-market currencies, though emerging markets show divergence.
- Corporate borrowing costs have increased, and bond prices have fallen (e.g., 10-year Treasury down ~4.5%), but default spreads narrowed for most rated bonds while widening for high-yield, showing selective risk repricing.
- Equities have risen 12–13% in 2026 despite higher rates because rising earnings expectations (up ~11% for 2026) have offset the discount-rate headwind; the effect is highly sector and company-specific (energy up 40%, tech up 25% but top-heavy, utilities/consumer down).
- Interest rate effects on stock value depend on *why* rates rose and how company cash flows respond (pricing power, cost pass-through, margins, reinvestment cuts); no single direction is guaranteed.
- Future rate direction hinges on inflation breakout, not Fed messaging; oil prices and sustained vs. transitory inflation will be the critical watch.

**Takeaway:** Higher interest rates in 2026 reflect anchored inflation expectations (~2.5–3%), not Fed decisions. While bond prices have fallen and corporate borrowing costs risen, equities have climbed on strong earnings growth; the relationship between rates and stock prices is complex and varies by company, requiring nuance beyond simple "higher rates = lower stocks" thinking.

**🔍 Reality check:** Damodaran argues Fed and Treasury actions matter less than inflation fundamentals—a structural claim that may understate near-term policy volatility or risk-asset correlation shifts. His earnings-growth explanation for equity resilience is sound but depends on Q3 earnings reports validating current consensus expectations, which he flags as uncertain. The claim that 2008–2021 was "abnormal" and 2026 is "normal" is historically grounded but partly framing-dependent; past experience doesn't guarantee future rate regimes. No major caveats on evidence quality or bias—measured and well-sourced."

**Materials:** Slides and two Excel data sets (treasury rates 1954–2026, intrinsic vs. actual rates, S&P sector/regional breakdowns, earnings forecasts) provide full transparency; blog post reinforces the FOMC-skepticism and inflation-focus narrative. High-quality supplementary materials strengthen credibility.
