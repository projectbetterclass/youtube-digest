## [How To Never Hear “No”](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/vETAteck9b0)

**Alex Hormozi** · 🟡 Skim
> A useful tactical reframe for sales and negotiation; the core idea is straightforward and captured by key points, so full video viewing adds little.

**Key points**
- Instead of yes/no binary questions, ask 'What would it take?' to get the hidden costs or constraints
- The prospect reveals their actual priorities and trade-offs (e.g., skipping meetings, deprioritizing other work)
- You retain decision-making power—you see the true cost and choose whether it's worth it, rather than the other party deciding for you
- Common use case: 'What would it take to get five videos edited by Friday?' vs. 'Can you get five videos edited by Friday?'

**Takeaway:** Reframe requests from "Can you do X?" to "What would it take to do X?" to bypass automatic rejection and force the other party to name the actual constraints or trade-offs, letting you make an informed decision rather than accepting their no.

**🔍 Reality check:** The technique assumes the other party will honestly reveal their constraints and trade-offs; in practice, some will still deflect, quote inflated prices, or decline without elaborating. It's not a universal key to never hearing 'no,' but rather a useful way to make rejections more informative. The framing also works best when you already have leverage or when the other party trusts you enough to be candid."

## [Vanessa Van Edwards: The Conversation Habit That Makes Smart People Unlikeable](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q2cg1gEYWJQ)

**The Diary Of A CEO** · ✅ Watch in full
> Rich, actionable research-backed framework with concrete techniques; the archetype breakdown and practical exercises (yap challenge, graceful exit) alone justify the 2+ hour runtime for anyone serious about communication.

**Key points**
- The three-level conversation framework: Level 1 (general facts) should focus on stories and emotion rather than autopilot questions like 'What do you do?'; best openers include 'What was the highlight of your day?' and 'Have any fun plans coming up?'
- Conversational mechanics matter: ideal turn-taking is 15–30 seconds; speaking over 45 seconds creates friction; oversharing non-verbally signals desire to speak (the 'open fish' cue).
- Five personality archetypes shape how people communicate: ambitious hero, contented pragmatist, selfless helper, creative disruptor, and unlucky struggler—recognizing these drives smarter interactions and relationship predictions.
- Charisma and 'aura' come from signaling genuine liking clearly, using your maximum resonance point (deepest vocal range), taking up physical space, and making others feel heard—not from trying to impress.
- Self-narratives (redemption vs. contamination) are learnable; asking 'Do you feel lucky?' reveals internal locus of control, and reframing how you story your experiences can shift your actual outcomes.
- Practical tools: the graceful exit (future question + compliment/action + exit voice), listening cues (lower lid flex = intense focus), and pre-scripting answers to questions you're asked repeatedly to offer conversational gifts.

**Takeaway:** 
Conversation is a learnable skill that builds careers, relationships, and well-being—apply a three-level framework (facts-to-stories, personal concerns, self-narrative), nail the mechanics (timing, body language, vocal tone), and recognize personality archetypes to connect authentically and break free from social autopilot.


**🔍 Reality check:** Van Edwards' research is mostly her own lab work and cited studies (notably Richard Wiseman on luck); the archetypes are described as art-informed rather than rigorously validated personality constructs. Some prescriptions (e.g., "best conversation starter was…") are based on limited speed-networking experiments with 500 people, not universal law. The claim that 95% of people have worked with someone who talks too much is real but statistically uninformative. Also: the book is being marketed throughout; listening is still transactional if your goal is extracting networking value rather than genuine connection. The interview format itself violates the 15–45 second turn rule Van Edwards describes, which she acknowledges—a useful reminder that rigid rules break in context.

**Materials:** Book pre-order: Conversation by Vanessa Van Edwards. Van Edwards' prior books (Cues, Captivate) and YouTube channel linked. No academic papers provided, but she references Dan McAdams' three-level conversation framework and Richard Wiseman's luck research—both are real. Diary of a CEO conversation cards available for purchase."
