## [I make $25K/month from one simple website](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xEoLsAQFnOY)

**Starter Story** · ✅ Watch in full
> Genuine case study with concrete metrics, specific tactics (content thrifting, template design, creator recruitment), and replicable playbook; uncommon emphasis on distribution over product innovation has practical value.

**Key points**
- Career Hound, a simple job board that scrapes company jobs, generates ~$25K/month from 779 active subscribers paying $7/week to $50/year; founder is non-technical and uses no-free-model strategy.
- Growth driven entirely by marketing and content distribution, not product differentiation—founder emphasizes finding timeless (repeatable) content formats rather than chasing trends.
- Core playbook: spend hours 'content thrifting' across social platforms (Instagram, Reddit, TikTok) to identify recurring patterns, then build repeatable content formats (e.g., 'companies hiring remotely worldwide').
- Recruits older content creators (50s+) via direct DM, paying ~$20 per video with heavily templated instructions; association with age/wisdom increases credibility for job-search content.
- Advice for founders: find winning product categories (via SideShift or Sensor Tower), clone a similar product, use competitors' content as marketing inspiration, then run daily marketing experiments and extract learnings from failures.

**Takeaway:** 
A boring job board makes $25K/month not because of product innovation, but because the founder mastered repeatable content marketing by identifying timeless formats and scaling them through affordable, templated creator partnerships. The lesson: proven categories + disciplined marketing + consistency beats novelty.


**🔍 Reality check:** Founder's $25K/month is real and claimed transparently, but context matters: (1) High churn (31%) suggests product is commoditized—not a moat; (2) success required ~2 years of experimentation and consistent daily work, not a quick win; (3) strategy depends on access to a large, engaged audience (11.7M social views/month), which is the bottleneck most builders will hit, not replicable in saturated spaces without differentiation; (4) job boards are indeed saturated, so finding an unserved angle or audience is harder than the framing suggests; (5) video is no guarantee—creator selection, format discovery, and audience insight still require significant trial-and-error; (6) no mention of unit economics (CAC, LTV relationship) or sustainability if platforms change algorithm or competition intensifies.

**Materials:** Creator's Twitter (@rlakhnyuk) and Career Hound site available for direct verification. Starter Story reference database (clickhubspot.com/m8ed) offers 50+ similar case studies for comparison; useful for finding other working ideas but not a guarantee model template.

> A short clip delivering one straightforward idea; useful reframing but brief enough that reading the key points captures most of the value.

**Key points**
- James Clear insight: desiring a result doesn't make sense if you're not willing to do what it takes to achieve it
- Core idea: if you don't want the lifestyle/process required to reach a goal, you should release the desire for that goal

The advice works best for introspection but underestimates how desires and motivation actually shift over time. ## [Can Video Games Help with Anxiety](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/YLmIv35RK50)

**HealthyGamerGG** · 🟡 Skim
> The core idea is practical but the video is a short anecdote from one clinician about one child; it captures the concept but lacks depth, peer research, or broader applicability guidance.

**Key points**
- Dr. K used horror-game sequences (Metroid Dread) to deliberately activate his daughter's anxiety after teaching her breathing meditation
- The game mechanics force emotional regulation—failure requires calm, focused decision-making to progress, creating natural incentive to stay regulated
- This is framed as exposure therapy: controlled 'nervous system activation' combined with practiced coping skills, rather than game play as direct therapy
- Dr. K positions this as counterintuitive parenting—reframing games as tools instead of anxiety triggers
- The method requires both prior skill teaching (meditation) and careful game selection; not all gaming serves this purpose

**Takeaway:** Video games with mild stress elements can serve as practical anxiety exposure training if paired with pre-learned coping techniques like breathing meditation, allowing kids to practice regulation in low-stakes situations.

**🔍 Reality check:** This is exposure-based anxiety training framed around one personal case, not clinical evidence. The implicit claim—that gaming with parents who know anxiety psychology can be therapeutic—relies heavily on parental skill, game fit, and prior coping training, none of which are guaranteed. No mention of which kids might not benefit or risks of overuse. This is clinical opinion presented engagingly, not validated protocol; results vary widely by individual.

**Materials:** Links provided are to Dr. K's broader content library (meditation, ADHD, trauma videos) and his coaching service; no peer-reviewed research or structured clinical evidence linked.

## [Can't feel rear delts? Do this.](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/f6CB9TioLss)

**Ben Yanes** · 🟡 Skim
> Clear, practical cue for a common problem with immediate application; the biomechanical reasoning is sound and the visual comparison makes the concept easy to grasp.

**Key points**
- Rear delt activation on reverse fly machines depends critically on torso angle and arm angle relative to the torso, not hand position in space.
- Sitting farther back on the seat creates a high-to-low pulling angle (downward), which shifts loading from scapular retraction to isolated rear delt activation.
- Horizontal pulling (middle-to-middle) requires significant scapular retraction; angled pulling (high-to-low) minimizes scapular involvement and maximizes rear delt work.
- The coach emphasizes analyzing the body's system (torso, arm angle) before focusing on external mechanics (where the handles are).

**Takeaway:** To feel rear delts on reverse fly machines, sit farther back to create a high-to-low pulling angle relative to your torso—this isolates the rear delts and reduces reliance on scapular retraction.</takeaway>
